<h2 id="how-to-choose">Safety rules for curing at home</h2>
<ul class="sk-checks"><li><strong>Follow a tested recipe</strong> from the kit maker or a trusted book; don't improvise salt or cure amounts.</li><li><strong>Weigh everything in grams,</strong> including the meat, on a digital scale.</li><li><strong>Use cure #2 for long dry cures</strong> and cure #1 for things you'll cook.</li><li><strong>Weigh the meat as it dries.</strong> Most cures are done when they've lost about a third of their starting weight.</li><li><strong>Know your molds.</strong> Even white mold is normal; fuzzy green or black mold, or slime, means throw it out.</li><li><strong>Skip home-cured meat</strong> for pregnant guests and anyone with a weakened immune system.</li></ul>

## Home curing questions

**Can you cure meat in a regular fridge?**

Yes, with breathable dry-aging bags like UMAi Dry, which let moisture out while keeping mold and odors away.

**What is the easiest charcuterie to make at home?**

Whole-muscle cuts like coppa, bresaola and lomo. Salami is harder because it needs grinding, a starter culture and fermentation.

**Do I need curing salt?**

Yes, for anything dried slowly. The nitrite in curing salt prevents botulism. Use the amount a tested recipe specifies.

**What is the difference between cure #1 and cure #2?**

Cure #1 contains nitrite and is for bacon and cooked sausages. Cure #2 adds nitrate, which releases nitrite slowly over a long dry cure.

**How do you know when cured meat is done?**

Weigh it. Most dry cures are ready when they have lost about a third of their starting weight.
