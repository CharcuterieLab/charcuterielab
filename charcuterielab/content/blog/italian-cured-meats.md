# Italian Cured Meats: A Region-by-Region Guide to Salumi

> **Quick Answer:** Italian cured meats are called **salumi**: whole-muscle hams like prosciutto, salami, cooked meats like mortadella, and cured fat like lardo. The north makes sweet, delicate hams, the center adds fennel and herbs, and the south adds chile.

Every region of Italy cures pork its own way, and the differences follow the map. The cool, dry north makes sweet, delicate hams. Central Italy adds fennel, pepper and herbs. The south adds chile. This guide goes region by region through 25 of the salumi you're most likely to find (or want to hunt down), then shows you how Italians serve them and how to build an Italian board at home.

For every cured meat from every country in one searchable chart, see our [100 types of cured meat](/blog/types-of-cured-meat/). This page is the deep dive on Italy.

<h2 id="kinds">Salumi in 30 seconds: the four kinds</h2>
:::

| Kind | In Italian | What it is | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Whole-muscle** | Salumi crudi interi | A whole cut, salted and air-dried, never ground | [Prosciutto](/ingredients/prosciutto-di-parma/), [culatello](/ingredients/culatello/), [coppa](/ingredients/coppa/), [bresaola](/ingredients/bresaola/), [speck](/ingredients/speck/) |
| **Salami** | Salame (plural salami) | Chopped meat and fat with spices, stuffed into a casing, fermented and dried | Felino, [finocchiona](/ingredients/finocchiona/), [soppressata](/ingredients/soppressata/), Milano |
| **Cooked** | Salumi cotti | Cooked during making, so it's moist and mild | [Mortadella](/ingredients/mortadella/), prosciutto cotto, porchetta |
| **Cured fat and spreads** | Lardo, pancetta, spalmabili | Cured fat to slice or cook, and salami soft enough to spread | [Lardo](/ingredients/lardo/), [guanciale](/ingredients/guanciale/), ['nduja](/ingredients/nduja/), ciauscolo |

**A word to know:** in Italy, *prosciutto* just means ham. Ask for *prosciutto crudo* for the raw, air-dried kind and *prosciutto cotto* for cooked ham.

<h2 id="northeast">The Alps and the northeast</h2>
<p class="sk-lede"><b>Alto Adige, Friuli and the Veneto.</b> Cold, dry mountain air and a little smoke. Sweet hams and soft salami.</p>
:::

Speck comes from Alto Adige (Südtirol), where the food is half Italian and half Austrian. Its motto is a little salt, a little smoke and plenty of fresh air. San Daniele is Parma's main rival for Italy's best ham. Try both side by side: San Daniele is usually a touch sweeter and softer.

<h2 id="lombardy">Lombardy and the northwest</h2>
<p class="sk-lede"><b>Lombardy, Piedmont and the Valtellina.</b> Lean beef and mild, finely ground salami.</p>
:::

Lombardy gives you bresaola, Milan's mild salami and the little hunter's salami found all over the north. The region also makes Salame di Varzi DOP and Salame Brianza DOP, which you'll rarely see in the US.

<h2 id="emilia">Emilia-Romagna: Italy's food valley</h2>
<p class="sk-lede"><b>Parma, Piacenza and Bologna.</b> The richest stretch of salumi in the world. Sweet hams, silky mortadella, marbled coppa.</p>
:::

The plain between Piacenza and Bologna is the same land that makes Parmigiano-Reggiano and balsamic vinegar of Modena. The pigs are fed partly on whey left over from Parmigiano, and the Po river fog helps the hams and culatello cure slowly. Piacenza alone has three DOP salumi: coppa, pancetta and salame.

<h2 id="dop">DOP and IGP: what the labels mean</h2>
:::

Italy has more protected food and wine names than any other country in Europe, and dozens of them are cured meats. Two EU labels do the work:

- **DOP** (*Denominazione di Origine Protetta*, or PDO in English): the raw ingredients come from the region and every step of production happens there. Examples: Prosciutto di Parma, San Daniele, culatello di Zibello, Coppa Piacentina.
- **IGP** (*Indicazione Geografica Protetta*, or PGI): at least one step of production, processing or preparation happens in the region. Examples: mortadella Bologna, bresaola della Valtellina, speck Alto Adige, finocchiona.
- **On the package:** the DOP seal is red and yellow; the IGP seal is blue and yellow. Prosciutto di Parma also carries a five-point ducal crown branded on the skin, and San Daniele carries an "SD" brand.
- **Without a seal:** "Italian-style", "Genoa" or "Calabrese" tells you the style, not where it was made. Look for "Product of Italy" if that matters to you. Many good US makers produce Italian-style salumi too.

:::html
<h2 id="serve">How Italians serve salumi</h2>
:::

In Italy a board of mixed sliced meats is called an *affettato misto*, served on a *tagliere* (a wooden cutting board) as an antipasto. A few habits make it taste better at home:

- **Slice it thin and serve it soon.** Prosciutto should be almost see-through. Have the deli slice it, lay it on parchment, and eat it within a day or two.
- **Take it out 20 to 30 minutes early.** Fat tastes of nothing when it's cold. Room temperature lets the sweetness come through. Don't leave it out more than 2 hours.
- **Go from mild to strong.** Start with mortadella, prosciutto and bresaola, then salami, then spicy soppressata or 'nduja.
- **Peel the salami.** Most casings on larger salami aren't meant to be eaten. Thin casings on small salami are fine.
- **Serve bread, not just crackers.** <a href="/ingredients/grissini/">Grissini</a>, focaccia, crusty bread, flatbreads or fried dough. See [the best bread for a charcuterie board](/blog/what-bread-for-charcuterie-board/).
- **Fold, don't fan.** Loose folds of prosciutto look generous and are easy to pick up. For a showpiece, try a [prosciutto rose](/blog/prosciutto-rose/) or a [salami rose](/blog/how-to-make-a-salami-rose/).

<h2 id="pairings">What goes with Italian cured meats</h2>
:::

The easy rule: pair salumi with the cheese, bread and wine from the same region. They've been eaten together for centuries.

| Meat | Cheese | Something sweet or fresh | Wine |
|---|---|---|---|
| Prosciutto di Parma | [Parmigiano-Reggiano](/pairings/food/parmigiano-reggiano/) | Cantaloupe or melon | Prosecco or Lambrusco |
| Culatello | Parmigiano-Reggiano | Sweet butter | Lambrusco |
| Mortadella | Fresh mozzarella | Pistachios | Lambrusco or Prosecco |
| Bresaola | Shaved Parmigiano | Arugula and lemon | Light red, like Pinot Noir |
| Speck | Fontina or Asiago | Apples | Pinot Noir or a dry white |
| Finocchiona | Pecorino toscano | Pears | Chianti |

<h2 id="buying">Buying Italian cured meats in the US</h2>

- **Deli counter or pre-sliced?** Freshly sliced wins for prosciutto, culatello and mortadella. Pre-sliced packs are fine for salami and convenient for a party.
- **Ask for it thin.** Paper-thin for prosciutto, bresaola and speck; about 1/16 inch for salami.
- **Read the label.** "Product of Italy" and a DOP or IGP seal mean imported and protected. "Italian-style" means made elsewhere in the style.
- **How long it keeps:** eat opened sliced meats within 3 to 5 days. A whole, uncut dry salami keeps for weeks in the fridge.

<h2 id="faq">Italian cured meats: FAQ</h2>
:::

**What are Italian cured meats called?**

Salumi. It's the Italian word for all cured and preserved meats, from prosciutto to mortadella. Salami (salame in Italian) is just one kind of salumi: the ground, stuffed and dried sausages.

**What is the most famous Italian cured meat?**

Prosciutto di Parma is the best known around the world, followed by mortadella from Bologna. Among Italians, culatello di Zibello is often called the finest, and it's the most expensive.

**What Italian cured meats are best for a charcuterie board?**

Pick three that taste different: a sweet ham (Prosciutto di Parma or San Daniele), a salami (Felino, finocchiona or Genoa) and something bold (spicy soppressata or 'nduja). Add bresaola if a guest doesn't eat pork. Plan about 2 ounces of meat per person when there's a meal, and 3 to 4 when the board is the meal.

**What is the difference between Genoa salami and Italian dry salami?**

Both are American labels for Italian-style salami, and recipes vary by brand. Genoa is usually the softer, milder one, seasoned with garlic, wine and whole peppercorns. Italian dry salami is usually drier, firmer and more peppery. Neither is a protected Italian name.

**What is gabagool?**

Gabagool is the Italian-American way of saying capocollo, the cured pork neck and shoulder that's also sold as coppa or capicola. The name comes from southern Italian dialects, which soften and clip the ends of words.

**Which Italian cured meats need to be cooked?**

Guanciale and most pancetta sold in the US are cured but still raw, so cook them first. Cotechino and zampone, the big holiday sausages from Modena, are also cooked before eating. Prosciutto, salami, coppa, bresaola, speck and mortadella are ready to eat.

**Are Italian cured meats safe during pregnancy?**

The CDC advises people who are pregnant to heat deli meats, including cured meats, to 165°F or until steaming hot before eating. Prosciutto or salami baked on a pizza is fine. Ask your doctor if you're unsure.

**What do DOP and IGP mean on Italian cured meats?**

They're EU protected names. DOP (PDO in English) means every step happens in the named region, with local raw ingredients. IGP (PGI) means at least one step of production happens there. Both come with written rules, inspections and a seal on the package.
