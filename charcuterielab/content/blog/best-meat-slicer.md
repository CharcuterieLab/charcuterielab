<h2 id="how-to-choose">What to look for in a home meat slicer</h2>
<ul class="sk-checks"><li><strong>A blade that hides at zero.</strong> When the thickness knob is at zero, the blade should be fully covered, so you can't nick yourself putting it away.</li><li><strong>A blade you can remove without touching the edge,</strong> and a removable carriage. Cleaning is the job you'll do most.</li><li><strong>7 to 7.5 inches</strong> handles salami, soppressata and most cheeses. Go to 10 inches only for big roasts and hams.</li><li><strong>For paper-thin salami,</strong> chill the meat in the freezer 20 to 30 minutes first. Never slice fully frozen meat.</li><li><strong>Prosciutto is the hard one:</strong> home slicers tear it, so buy prosciutto pre-sliced and use the slicer for salami, ham, turkey breast and cheese.</li><li><strong>Clean it every time:</strong> unplug it, wear a cut-resistant glove, wipe the blade from the center out, and dry it right away.</li></ul>

## Meat slicer questions

**What is the best meat slicer for home use?**

The Cuisinart Kitchen Pro FS-75 won Your Best Digs' test; the Chef'sChoice 615A was Food & Wine's best overall. Both are 7-inch-class slicers for about $130.

**Is a meat slicer worth it for charcuterie?**

Yes, if you buy whole salami, ham or cheese regularly: a whole chub or block usually costs less per pound than pre-sliced, and you get the thickness you want.

**Can a home slicer cut prosciutto?**

Not well. Prosciutto is too soft and thin, and home slicers tend to tear it. Buy it pre-sliced.

**What size blade do I need?**

7 to 7.5 inches for salami and cheese; 10 inches if you slice large roasts or hams.

**How do you clean a meat slicer safely?**

Unplug it, set the thickness to zero, wear a cut-resistant glove, remove the blade and carriage, wash and dry everything right away.
