# Beaujolais Nouveau and Charcuterie: Why This Light Red Works

> **Quick Answer:** Beaujolais Nouveau 2026 goes on sale **Thursday, November 19**. It's one of the easiest wines you can pour with charcuterie: **low in tannin, high in acid, juicy with red fruit, and served cool.** Pair it with salami, pâté, ham and soft French cheeses, and skip the strongest blues.

## Beaujolais Nouveau at a glance

| | |
|---|---|
| 2026 release | Thursday, November 19, 12:01 a.m. (the third Thursday of November, set by French law) |
| Grape | Gamay |
| How it's made | Whole-berry (carbonic) fermentation and bottling six to eight weeks after harvest |
| Taste | Bright red cherry, raspberry, banana and bubblegum notes; light body; almost no tannin |
| Serve at | About 55°F (13°C): 20 to 30 minutes in the fridge |
| Drink by | Spring. It's made to drink young. |

## Why it works with charcuterie

Four things make Nouveau a natural match for a board.

**1. Almost no tannin.** Tannin is the drying, grippy feel in big reds like Cabernet. With salty cured meat it can taste harsh, and next to fish or very salty bites it can turn metallic. Nouveau's whole-berry fermentation keeps the tannin low, so there's nothing to clash with.

**2. High acid.** Acid makes your mouth water, and saliva rinses fat off your tongue. A sip after a slice of saucisson works like a cornichon: the next bite tastes fresh. That's the [acid rule](/blog/acid-rule/) in a glass.

**3. Juicy fruit against salt.** Salt makes fruit taste fruitier, and fruit softens salt. It's the same reason prosciutto and melon works. More on that in [the salt-sweet principle](/blog/salt-sweet-principle/).

**4. It's served cool.** At 55°F the wine feels lighter and crisper than a room-temperature red, which helps on a rich board. Take the cheese out to warm up and put the wine in to chill at the same time. See our [temperature guide](/blog/temperature-guide/).

## What to put on a Beaujolais Nouveau board

Beaujolais sits just north of Lyon, the French city famous for charcuterie. A Lyon-style board is the classic match.

**Meat**
- [Rosette de Lyon](/ingredients/rosette-de-lyon/): Lyon's own dry sausage, mild, porky and garlicky.
- [Saucisson sec](/ingredients/saucisson-sec/): the everyday French salami.
- [Jambon de Bayonne](/ingredients/jambon-de-bayonne/) or [prosciutto](/ingredients/prosciutto-di-parma/): thin, silky and salty.
- [Pâté de campagne](/ingredients/pate-de-campagne/) or [rillettes](/ingredients/rillettes/): rich spreads that the wine's acid cuts through.

**Cheese**
- [Brie](/ingredients/brie/) or [Camembert](/ingredients/camembert/): creamy, buttery and mild.
- [Reblochon](/ingredients/reblochon/) or [Morbier](/ingredients/morbier/): soft, nutty Alpine cheeses.
- A young [Comté](/ingredients/comte/): sweet and nutty. Skip the very old ones (24 months and up), which can overwhelm a wine this light.
- [Fresh goat cheese](/ingredients/fresh-chevre/): tangy and bright, matching the wine's acid.

**Everything else**
- [Cornichons](/ingredients/cornichons/) and [Dijon mustard](/ingredients/dijon-mustard/)
- A [baguette](/ingredients/baguette/), sliced thin
- [Pears](/ingredients/pears/) and red [grapes](/ingredients/grapes/)
- [Dried cherries](/ingredients/dried-cherries/), which echo the wine's fruit
- [Roasted chestnuts](/ingredients/roasted-chestnuts/), for a November touch

## What to skip

- **Very strong blue cheese** like Roquefort or Stilton. They bury the wine's fresh fruit. Save them for [port](/blog/best-port-wine/).
- **Stinky washed-rind cheese** like Époisses or Limburger. Same problem.
- **Very sweet jams and honey in big amounts.** Sweet food makes a dry wine taste thin and sour. A little fig jam is fine; a pool of honey isn't.
- **Heavily smoked meat.** Smoke flattens the wine's fruit.

Put the pâté and the Brie at opposite ends of the board, with the cornichons and mustard near the meat. Slice the baguette last so it stays soft. For a full French layout, see our [French brasserie board](/boards/french-brasserie-board/).

## After the Nouveau: cru Beaujolais

Nouveau is the party wine. Once it's gone, look for the **cru Beaujolais** villages on the label: **Fleurie, Morgon, Brouilly, Moulin-à-Vent.** They're the same Gamay grape, but with more depth, and they pair with the same board. They also keep well past spring.

## Bring it to Thanksgiving

## FAQ

**When is Beaujolais Nouveau released in 2026?**

Thursday, November 19, 2026. By French law it goes on sale at 12:01 a.m. on the third Thursday of November, so the US gets it the same day, one week before Thanksgiving.

**What food goes with Beaujolais Nouveau?**

Salty, fatty and simple foods: saucisson sec, rosette de Lyon, jambon de Bayonne, pâté de campagne and rillettes, plus soft and mild cheeses like Brie, Reblochon, Morbier and young Comté. Add cornichons, Dijon mustard and a baguette.

**What cheese pairs with Beaujolais Nouveau?**

Soft and semi-soft cheeses with mild to medium flavor: Brie, Camembert, Reblochon, Morbier, young Comté and fresh goat cheese. Skip very strong blues and stinky washed-rind cheeses, which bury the wine's fresh fruit.

**Should Beaujolais Nouveau be served chilled?**

Yes, lightly. Aim for about 55°F (13°C): 20 to 30 minutes in the fridge if it's been at room temperature. Cool, it tastes fresher and juicier, and it cuts through fatty meat better.

**How long does Beaujolais Nouveau last?**

It's made to drink young. It's bottled six to eight weeks after harvest and is best within a few months, so plan to finish it by spring. It doesn't improve with age.

**Is Beaujolais Nouveau good for Thanksgiving?**

Yes. It arrives a week before Thanksgiving, and its low tannin and bright acid work with turkey, cranberry sauce and a pre-dinner charcuterie board without overpowering anything.
