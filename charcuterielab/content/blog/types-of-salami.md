# Types of Salami: 13 Styles and How to Pick Them for a Board

> **Quick Answer:** The main types: **Genoa, hard salami, soppressata, calabrese, finocchiona, 'nduja and pepperoni**, plus Spanish chorizo and fuet. For a board, buy three: **one mild, one spicy, one unusual**.

## What makes a salami a salami?

Salami is a method, not a single sausage. Ground meat and fat are seasoned with salt and spices, stuffed into a casing, fermented, then hung to dry until firm enough to slice.

Everything else changes by region: the spice, the grind, the size of the casing, whether it's smoked. That's why one country can make a dozen salamis that taste nothing alike.

<h2 id="italian">Italian salami types</h2>
:::

**Genoa salami** is the one most Americans know. It's pork-based, seasoned with garlic and pepper and often wine, with a soft bite and a gentle tang. It's the easiest meat to put on any board. See the [Genoa salami guide](/ingredients/genoa-salami/) and [what goes with Genoa salami](/pairings/food/genoa-salami/).

**Soppressata** is coarser and more rustic, and comes two ways: *dolce* (sweet, meaning not spicy) and *piccante* (hot). The hot version is the one people remember. Our [soppressata guide](/blog/soppressata/) covers both.

**Calabrese** comes from Calabria, the toe of Italy's boot, and gets its heat from Calabrian chili. It's the Italian salami to buy if you like pepperoni.

**Finocchiona** is Tuscan and scented with fennel seed. It tastes faintly sweet and aromatic, which makes it the best salami for fruit. More in the [finocchiona guide](/ingredients/finocchiona/).

**Felino** is named for a town near Parma. It's mild and delicate, the kind of salami you taste the pork in.

**Cacciatore** means "hunter's" salami. It's small and compact, mild and garlicky, and its size makes it easy to slice into neat coins.

**'Nduja** is the outlier: a soft, spreadable Calabrian salami loaded with chili. You don't slice it; you serve it in a ramekin with a spoon. See [the 'nduja guide](/ingredients/nduja/).

:::html
<h2 id="american">American salami types</h2>
:::

**Hard salami** is the American deli style. It's drier and firmer than Genoa, often made with pork and beef, often smoked, and mild. It holds its shape on a cracker and in a sandwich.

**Pepperoni** is America's own salami: finely ground, seasoned with paprika and chili, and usually smoked. It's the spiciest thing on most casual boards. For the full comparison, read [salami vs. pepperoni](/blog/salami-vs-pepperoni/).

:::html
<h2 id="spanish-french">Spanish and French salami types</h2>
:::

**Chorizo (Spanish)** is cured and dried like salami and seasoned with pimentón, Spanish paprika, which turns it brick red. It comes *dulce* (sweet) or *picante* (hot). Don't confuse it with Mexican chorizo, which is a fresh sausage you cook.

**Salchichón** is Spain's black-pepper salami. Think of it as chorizo without the paprika: pinker, milder and peppery.

**Fuet** is a thin, firm Catalan salami, often with a white bloom on the casing. It's mild and clean-tasting, and it snaps into neat little coins. See the [fuet guide](/ingredients/fuet/).

**Saucisson sec** simply means "dry sausage" in French. It's pork, salt, pepper and often garlic, usually with a powdery white mold on the casing that's edible. The [saucisson sec guide](/ingredients/saucisson-sec/) has the details.

:::html
<h2 id="spiciest">Which salami is spiciest?</h2>
:::

From hottest to mildest, roughly:

1. **'Nduja**
2. **Calabrese** and **hot soppressata**
3. **Hot chorizo** and **pepperoni**
4. **Everything else:** Genoa, hard salami, felino, cacciatore, finocchiona, salchichón, fuet, saucisson sec and sweet chorizo

Heat varies by producer, so taste before you plan a board around it.

:::html
<h2 id="pick">How to pick 2-3 salamis for a board</h2>
:::

Use one rule: **each salami should do a different job.**

- **The mild anchor.** Genoa, felino or hard salami. Everyone eats it, including kids.
- **The heat.** Calabrese, hot soppressata or 'nduja. Put it next to something creamy.
- **The wildcard.** Finocchiona for fennel, fuet or saucisson sec for a different shape and texture.

Match the rest of the board to the salami's country. Chorizo and fuet with Manchego and almonds; finocchiona with Pecorino and pears; Genoa and calabrese with mozzarella and olives.

**How much to buy:** the site standard is 2 oz of meat per guest as an appetizer. For 8 guests that's 1 lb total, for example 6 oz Genoa, 5 oz calabrese and 5 oz finocchiona. Double it if the board is the meal.

:::html
<h2 id="storage">How long does salami last?</h2>
:::

A whole dry salami is shelf-stable in the fridge. The [University of Minnesota Extension](https://extension.umn.edu/preserving-and-preparing/storage-times-food-refrigerator-and-freezer) puts hard or dry sausage at indefinitely unopened in the refrigerator, 3 weeks once opened, and 1-2 months in the freezer.

Salami sliced for you at the deli counter counts as deli meat: use it within 3-5 days.

<h2 id="faq">Common questions</h2>
:::

**What are the main types of salami?**

The ones you'll see most are Genoa, American hard salami, soppressata, calabrese, finocchiona, felino, cacciatore, 'nduja and pepperoni, plus Spanish chorizo, salchichón and fuet and French saucisson sec.

**What is the mildest salami?**

Genoa, hard salami, felino, cacciatore, saucisson sec, fuet and salchichón are all mild. Genoa is the safest pick for a mixed crowd.

**What is the spiciest salami?**

'Nduja is the hottest common salami. Among sliceable ones, calabrese and hot soppressata are the spiciest, followed by hot chorizo and pepperoni.

**Is chorizo a type of salami?**

Spanish chorizo is a cured, dried, sliceable sausage seasoned with paprika, so it works like a salami on a board. Mexican chorizo is a fresh sausage that must be cooked, so it doesn't.

**How many types of salami should I put on a board?**

Two or three. Pick one mild, one spicy and one with a different flavor or shape, such as fennel-scented finocchiona or a thin, firm fuet.
