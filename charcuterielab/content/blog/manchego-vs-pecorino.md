# Manchego vs. Pecorino: What's the Difference?

> **Quick Answer:** Both are sheep's milk cheeses. **Manchego** (Spain) is nutty and buttery. **Pecorino Romano** (Italy) is hard and very salty, mostly for grating. Want Italy's closest match to Manchego? **Pecorino Toscano**.

## First: "pecorino" is a family, not one cheese

*Pecora* is Italian for sheep, so pecorino means sheep's-milk cheese. Italy makes several protected versions, and the two you'll see most in the US are nothing alike:

- **Pecorino Romano DOP:** hard, dry and intensely salty. It's the grating cheese for cacio e pepe and carbonara.
- **Pecorino Toscano DOP:** softer, buttery and mild. It's a table cheese, made to eat in slices.

So the honest comparison is three cheeses, not two.

## Manchego vs. pecorino at a glance

| | Manchego | Pecorino Romano | Pecorino Toscano |
|---|---|---|---|
| Country | Spain (La Mancha) | Italy (mostly Sardinia, also Lazio) | Italy (Tuscany) |
| Milk | Manchega sheep | Sheep | Sheep |
| Aging | 60 days to 2 years (30 days for small wheels) | At least 5 months | 20 days to several months |
| Salt | Moderate | Very high | Low to moderate |
| Texture | Firm, slightly crumbly | Hard, dry, granular | Semi-firm to firm, buttery |
| Taste | Nutty, buttery, faintly tangy | Salty, sharp, tangy | Mild, sweet, milky |
| Rind | Zigzag basket pattern | Smooth, often black or dark | Pale yellow to reddish |
| Best use | Board star | Grating, cooking | Board cheese |

## Why they taste so different

**Salt is the main reason.** Pecorino Romano is dry-salted over and over for weeks, so it ends up much saltier than even Parmigiano-Reggiano. Manchego and Pecorino Toscano are salted far more lightly.

**Aging is the second.** Manchego changes a lot with age:

- **Semicurado** (about 3 months): soft, mild, milky.
- **Curado** (3 to 6 months): firm and nutty. The everyday board choice.
- **Viejo** (1 to 2 years): dry, sharp and crystalline.

Sheep's milk gives all three a little tang and a richer feel than cow's-milk cheeses, because it has more fat and protein.

## Which goes on a charcuterie board?

**Manchego or Pecorino Toscano. Use Romano for cooking.**

**Manchego** is the classic Spanish board cheese. Serve it in thin triangles with:

- **Membrillo** (quince paste), the traditional Spanish pairing.
- **Jamón serrano** and **Spanish chorizo**. See [chorizo and Manchego](/blog/chorizo-manchego-pairing/).
- **Marcona almonds** and green olives.
- **Drinks:** Tempranillo, Rioja or a dry sherry.

**Pecorino Toscano** goes with honey, pears, walnuts and fresh fava beans in spring, plus a Chianti.

**Pecorino Romano** on a board needs something sweet beside it: honey, fig jam or fresh pears. Shave it thin and put out only a little. Most guests find a whole wedge too salty.

Take Manchego and Toscano out of the fridge 30 to 45 minutes before serving; Romano and aged viejo Manchego can go 45 to 60.

## Substitutions

- **Cooking with Romano but only have Manchego?** Use aged Manchego, grate a bit more and add a pinch of salt.
- **Want a Manchego swap for an Italian board?** Pecorino Toscano stagionato (the aged one).
- **Grating cheese without the salt punch?** Parmigiano-Reggiano. See [Parmesan vs. Parmigiano-Reggiano](/blog/parmesan-vs-parmigiano-reggiano/).

## How much to buy

Plan on 2 oz of cheese per guest before a meal, 3 oz when the board is the party food and 4 oz when it's dinner, split across two or three cheeses. If Romano is on the board, count it as a garnish, about half an ounce per guest.

## Which should you buy?

- **For a Spanish or tapas board:** Manchego curado, about 6 months.
- **For a cheese-focused board:** Manchego viejo, 12 months or more.
- **For an Italian board:** Pecorino Toscano.
- **For pasta:** Pecorino Romano.
