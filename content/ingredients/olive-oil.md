## The short version

There are two olive oils in a good kitchen: the one you cook with, and the one you
finish with. The finishing oil is grassy, peppery and expensive, and its whole job
is being poured cold over things. On a board it's what turns burrata from a ball
of cheese into a plate.

## What it is

Extra virgin olive oil — mechanically pressed, unrefined, with free acidity below
0.8%. Character varies enormously by variety and region: Tuscan oils are grassy
and pepper-hot; Ligurian are mild and buttery; Greek and Spanish sit between.

**Taste:** grassy, peppery, faintly bitter, sometimes almond or artichoke
**Texture:** viscous and coating
**Look:** green-gold, cloudy if unfiltered

The mechanism is oleocanthal — the compound responsible for the peppery catch at
the back of the throat in a fresh oil. It's an irritant in the same family as
ibuprofen, and that slight burn is what makes a good finishing oil register as an
ingredient rather than a lubricant. Cooking destroys it, which is why finishing
oils are used cold.

## What it pairs with — and why

| Pair it with | Why it works |
|---|---|
| **Burrata** | The pairing that justifies the bottle. Cream with no seasoning meets fat with flavour, plus flaky salt. |
| **Fresh mozzarella and ricotta** | Same logic. Very mild cheeses are a canvas for oil. |
| **Baguette and crusty bread** | Oil, salt, bread. The Mediterranean's whole argument. |
| **Flaky sea salt** | They're used together every time. Neither works as well alone. |
| **Tomatoes and ripe fruit** | Oil, salt and acid on something sweet. |
| **Hard aged cheese** | Drizzle over Parmigiano shards. Unexpected, and very good. |

**Skip it with:** anything already oily — marinated artichokes, sun-dried
tomatoes, oil-cured olives. They bring their own.

## How to prep it for the board

1. **Buy a separate finishing bottle.** Cooking oil in a big tin is a different
   product with a different job.
2. **Pour it into a small bowl** for dipping, or drizzle it directly over the
   cheese.
3. **Drizzle from height** so it spreads rather than pooling in one spot.
4. **Use it cold, always.** Heat destroys the peppery compounds you paid for.
5. **Finish with flaky salt** immediately after the oil.
6. **Buy a dark bottle or tin** and keep it out of light and heat.
7. **Fix:** rancid, crayon-like smell? Oil goes off within a year of pressing. Buy
   small bottles and check the harvest date.

## What to buy

| Product | Where | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Tuscan or Umbrian extra virgin** | Italian delis, specialty | Grassy and peppery. The classic finishing style. |
| **Ligurian (Taggiasca) oil** | Italian delis | Mild, buttery, delicate. Better on fish and fresh cheese. |
| **Greek Koroneiki oil** | Greek grocers, specialty | Robust and green. Excellent value. |
| **California Olive Ranch** | Grocery | Widely available, honest, reasonably priced. Good everyday finishing oil. |
| **New-harvest / olio nuovo** | Specialty, Nov–Jan | Cloudy, intensely green and peppery. Seasonal and remarkable. |

**Label check:** look for a **harvest date**, not just a best-before. Extra virgin
in a dark glass bottle or tin, single-origin if possible.

**Skip:** anything in a clear plastic bottle, and anything labelled "light" or
"pure" olive oil — those are refined and have no flavour.

## Quick FAQ

**What's a finishing olive oil?**
An extra virgin oil used cold, poured over food rather than cooked with. Grassier,
more peppery, and more expensive than cooking oil.

**Why does good olive oil burn my throat?**
Oleocanthal, a compound in fresh oil. The peppery catch is a sign of quality, not
a fault.

**How do I know if olive oil is fresh?**
Look for a harvest date on the label. Olive oil is at its best within a year of
pressing.

**What do I drizzle it on?**
Burrata, fresh mozzarella, ricotta, bread, and hard aged cheese. Always with flaky
salt.

**Should I cook with my finishing oil?**
No — heat destroys the compounds you're paying for. Keep a cheaper oil for the pan.
