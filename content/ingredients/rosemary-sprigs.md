## The short version

The most-used garnish on every charcuterie board photograph, and for good reason.
Rosemary is stiff enough to hold its shape, dark enough to read against pale
cheese, and it fills the awkward gaps that make a board look sparse. It also
smells like the board is being cooked, which is more than a decorative herb
usually manages.

## What it is

Woody sprigs of *Salvia rosmarinus* — needle leaves on a stiff stem. Sold in
plastic clamshells in every supermarket, or cut from a plant that survives almost
any neglect. On a board it's used whole and mostly uneaten.

**Taste:** piney, resinous, camphorous, assertive
**Texture:** stiff needles on a woody stem
**Look:** dark blue-green needles, four to six inch sprigs

The mechanism is aroma without contact. Rosemary's oils are volatile enough that
guests smell the board before they taste anything, which primes them for savoury
food. Structurally, it's the only cheap ingredient stiff enough to bridge a gap
between two piles without collapsing — so it does presentation work no soft herb
can.

## What it pairs with — and why

| Pair it with | Why it works |
|---|---|
| **Manchego** | Rosemary and sheep milk is a Spanish combination, and some manchegos are aged in rosemary. |
| **Marcona almonds** | Rosemary-dusted marconas are sold as a product. Tucking a sprig beside the bowl reinforces it. |
| **Spiced nut mix** | Warm rosemary nuts are the reference recipe; the sprig signals what's in the bowl. |
| **Aged gouda** | Caramel and pine are further apart than they sound and work well. |
| **Baguette and focaccia** | Rosemary focaccia. The sprig belongs beside the bread. |
| **Everything, visually** | Gaps between cheeses, the edge of the board, under a wedge — this is the actual use. |

**Skip it with:** nothing, but don't let guests think it's food. It's tough and
resinous eaten raw.

## How to prep it for the board

1. **Rinse and dry the sprigs** thoroughly. Wet herbs bead water onto cheese.
2. **Tuck them into gaps** between piles — that's the job. Two or three sprigs
   is plenty on a standard board.
3. **Run your fingers along a sprig** before laying it down. Bruising the needles
   releases the oils and the board smells of it.
4. **Lay them under the edge of a wedge** so they look placed rather than dropped.
5. **Keep them off the crackers.** Needles fall.
6. **Don't over-garnish.** Four sprigs looks intentional; twelve looks like a
   hedge.
7. **Fix:** droopy? Stand the sprigs in cold water for ten minutes and they
   stiffen back up.

## What to buy

| Product | Where | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Fresh rosemary clamshell** | Any grocery | A dollar or two, lasts two weeks in the fridge. |
| **A rosemary plant** | Garden centre, grocery | Almost unkillable. Cheaper than three clamshells and always on hand. |
| **Fresh thyme** | Grocery | Softer and finer. Better scattered than laid. Has its own page. |
| **Bay leaves (fresh)** | Specialty, farmers markets | Glossy and dramatic. An underused alternative. |

**Label check:** the needles should be flexible, not dry and shedding. Brown tips
mean it's been in the case too long.

**Skip:** dried rosemary for garnish — it just looks like debris.

## Quick FAQ

**Can you eat rosemary sprigs on a charcuterie board?**
You can, but they're tough and resinous raw. On a board they're a garnish and an
aromatic, not something to eat.

**How many sprigs do I need?**
Two or three on a standard board. Enough to fill gaps, not enough to look like
landscaping.

**How do I make the board smell of rosemary?**
Run your fingers firmly along the needles before laying the sprig down. Bruising
releases the oils.

**Where do I put them?**
In the gaps between piles, and tucked under the edge of a cheese wedge so they
look placed.

**How long does fresh rosemary keep?**
About two weeks in the fridge in its clamshell, or indefinitely on a plant.
