## The short version

A thin Italian-style cracker made with real olive oil. Like a butter cracker,
it brings its own fat — but olive oil tastes fruity and peppery rather than
creamy, so it points a board toward tomatoes, fresh cheese and cured meat
instead of cheddar and sausage.

## What it is

The Italian *croccantino* tradition: flour, water, extra virgin olive oil,
salt, sometimes a little yeast. Rolled thin and baked hot. The dough is closer
to flatbread than to a snack cracker, which is why the good ones blister and
taste faintly of bread.

**Taste:** fruity, peppery, savory, lightly salted
**Texture:** thin, blistered, shatters cleanly
**Look:** pale gold, bubbled surface, irregular edges

The fat is the mechanism, and it's a different fat than a butter cracker's.
Good extra virgin olive oil carries polyphenols that read as grassy and
slightly peppery at the back of the throat — flavor rather than richness. That
peppery finish is what makes it work with tomato and mozzarella, which need
seasoning more than they need cream.

## What it pairs with — and why

| Pair it with | Why it works |
|---|---|
| **Burrata and fresh mozzarella** | Milky and mild needs salt and pepper. The cracker supplies both without adding a competing flavor. |
| **Ripe tomato with flaky salt** | The tomato-mozzarella-olive-oil triangle, with the cracker doing the oil's job. |
| **Prosciutto and mortadella** | Fatty cured pork against a fruity oil. The same reason prosciutto is served with olive-oil bread in Italy. |
| **Aged parmesan and pecorino** | Salty crystalline cheese and peppery oil. A classic Italian close. |
| **Pesto and tapenade** | Both are already olive-oil emulsions. The cracker is the same ingredient in solid form. |
| **Marinated olives and artichokes** | The whole antipasto plate lives in this flavor register. |
| **Ricotta with honey and black pepper** | Sweet, creamy, peppery. The cracker's finish ties it together. |

**Skip it with:** strong blue cheese, which flattens the oil's subtlety
entirely, and dessert boards — olive oil and chocolate is a restaurant dish,
not a party one.

## How to prep it for the board

1. **Break the large sheets, snap the small ones.** These are meant to look
   hand-broken.
2. **Serve 4–5 per person.** They're thin and light, and they disappear.
3. **Build the Italian corner around them** — mozzarella, tomato, prosciutto,
   olives — so the flavor logic is visible on the board.
4. **Set out a small dish of the same olive oil** for dipping. It costs nothing
   and it makes the pairing explicit.
5. **Keep them away from wet cheese.** Thin crackers plus fresh mozzarella's
   liquid is a five-minute problem.
6. **Fix:** softened crackers come back at 300°F for 3 minutes, and the heat
   also wakes the oil's aroma back up.

**Label check:** "extra virgin olive oil" high in the ingredient list. "Made
with olive oil" often means a sunflower or canola blend with a splash of olive
for the label.

**Skip:** anything listing palm oil. It's cheaper, it's flavorless, and it
leaves a waxy finish that dulls the cheese.

## Quick FAQ

**How is this different from a butter cracker?**
Same idea, different fat. Butter reads creamy and rounds off sharp flavors;
olive oil reads fruity and peppery and adds seasoning instead.

**What cheese goes best with olive oil crackers?**
Fresh cheeses — burrata, mozzarella, ricotta — then aged parmesan and pecorino.

**How many per person?**
4–5. They're thin and people take several.

**Are croccantini and olive oil crackers the same thing?**
Croccantini is a brand-style name for the Italian version. Same category.

**Can I make them?**
Yes, and easily — flour, olive oil, water, salt, rolled very thin, 400°F for
10 minutes. One of the best cracker projects for a beginner.
