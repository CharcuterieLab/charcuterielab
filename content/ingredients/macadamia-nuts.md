## The short version

The most expensive common nut and the richest — roughly 75% fat, with a soft,
almost creamy break. Macadamias don't crunch so much as give way. Use half an
ounce a head, put them somewhere they'll be noticed, and don't try to make them
the board's crunch element.

## What it is

The seed of an Australian tree, grown commercially in Australia, Hawaii and South
Africa. Sold roasted, salted, and sometimes honey-coated. The shell is the
hardest of any nut, which is most of why they cost what they do.

**Taste:** sweet, buttery, faintly coconut, mildly milky
**Texture:** dense and soft; breaks rather than snaps
**Look:** pale ivory spheres, uniform and glossy

The mechanism is pure fat with no bitterness. Where a walnut cuts through
richness, a macadamia adds to it — which sounds like a flaw and isn't, on a board
where the cheese is sharp or the fruit is acidic. It works like a small piece of
extra cheese, extending the finish rather than resetting it.

## What it pairs with — and why

| Pair it with | Why it works |
|---|---|
| **Brie and soft-ripened cheese** | Fat on fat, with the nut's faint sweetness reading like a cracker's would. Unusual and good. |
| **Aged gouda** | Butterscotch against buttery. Both sweet, neither sharp. |
| **Dried mango and pineapple** | Tropical logic, and the fruit's acid is what the nut is missing. |
| **Fresh goat cheese** | Tang cutting fat directly — the clearest contrast a macadamia gets. |
| **Dark chocolate** | The classic confection. Dessert corner. |
| **Honey** | Drizzled, with sharp cheese. Very rich, and a small amount goes far. |

**Skip it with:** other rich nuts. Macadamias plus cashews plus brie is a board
with no edges.

## How to prep it for the board

1. **No prep.** Buy roasted and salted.
2. **Serve in a small bowl** and keep the quantity modest — they're expensive
   and very rich.
3. **Halve them** if you want them to go further; the cut face looks clean.
4. **Serve 0.5 oz per person.** Nobody needs more.
5. **Put something acidic nearby** — citrus, tart fruit, a sharp cheese.
6. **Keep them cold.** Very high fat means fast rancidity.
7. **Fix:** bland? Under-salted macadamias taste of very little. Toss with flaky
   salt and warm briefly.

## What to buy

| Product | Where | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Roasted salted macadamias** | Grocery, Trader Joe's, Costco | The default. Costco is the best value by a distance. |
| **Hawaiian macadamias (Mauna Loa)** | Grocery, gift shops | The famous ones. Good, and priced for tourists. |
| **Australian macadamias** | Specialty, online | Often fresher and cheaper than Hawaiian. |
| **Honey-roasted macadamias** | Grocery | Dessert corner. Very sweet. |
| **Raw macadamias** | Health stores | Milder and softer. Toast them lightly at 300°F for 6 minutes. |

**Label check:** they should smell sweet and milky. Rancid macadamias smell like
crayons and it happens fast — check the date and buy small.

**Skip:** macadamia pieces, and anything that's been sitting in a warm shop.

## Quick FAQ

**Why are macadamia nuts so expensive?**
The shell is extremely hard and the trees take years to bear. Processing costs
drive the price.

**Are macadamias good on a cheese board?**
Yes, in small quantities. They're very rich, so they work as a luxury accent
rather than the board's main nut.

**What goes with macadamia nuts?**
Brie, aged gouda, tropical dried fruit, fresh goat cheese, and dark chocolate.

**How much per person?**
Half an ounce. They're rich enough that people take two or three.

**How do I store them?**
In the fridge or freezer. Their fat content makes them go rancid quickly.
