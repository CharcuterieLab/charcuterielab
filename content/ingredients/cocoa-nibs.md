## The short version

Cacao beans, fermented, roasted and cracked into pieces. This is chocolate before
sugar and milk are added, so it is bitter, roasted and completely dry. On a dessert
board it is the crunch and the bitterness that everything sweet is missing, and a
small bag lasts through a dozen boards.

## What it is

The raw material of all chocolate. After harvest the beans ferment in their pulp,
which develops the flavour precursors; roasting develops them further; cracking and
winnowing removes the husk. What remains is roughly half cocoa butter by weight,
which is why nibs feel rich despite being dry.

**Taste:** deeply bitter, roasted, faintly fruity, with no sweetness at all
**Texture:** hard, crisp, snapping like a coffee bean
**Look:** dark red-brown irregular chips

The mechanism is bitterness as a reset. Sugar and fat both coat the palate, and a
dessert board goes flat by the third bite because nothing clears them. Bitter
compounds do clear them — which is why a scatter of nibs over something sweet makes
the next mouthful taste as sharp as the first.

## What it pairs with — and why

| Pair it with | Why it works |
|---|---|
| **Dark chocolate** | The finished product beside the raw one. It shows people what chocolate actually is. |
| **Mascarpone** | Sweet cream with bitter crunch is the whole idea in two ingredients. |
| **Medjool dates** | Caramel-sweet and soft. Nibs pressed into a split date is close to a confection. |
| **Honey** | Drizzle first, scatter second. The nibs stick and the bitterness cuts the sugar. |
| **Chocolate hazelnut spread** | Adds the texture and the bitterness the spread lacks. |
| **Dried cherries** | Fruit acid, chocolate bitterness. The classic black forest logic. |

**Skip it with:** cured meat and brined items. Nibs are a dessert-corner
ingredient only.

## How to prep it for the board

1. **Scatter, do not pile.** A mound of nibs is unpleasant to eat.
2. **Put them on something soft or sweet** so the bitterness has a partner.
3. **Toast them for two minutes** in a dry pan if they smell flat. It wakes them up.
4. **Crush some coarsely** if you want them to stick to a spread.
5. **Serve 0.25 oz per person.**
6. **Keep them in the dessert corner**, well away from cheese.
7. **Fix:** too bitter for the room? Toss them with a spoonful of honey and a pinch
   of salt. They become a candied crunch and everyone eats them.

## What to buy

| Product | Where | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Roasted cocoa nibs** | Health food shops, specialty grocers | The default. Deep, roasted, reliably bitter. |
| **Raw cacao nibs** | Health food shops | Fruitier and more acidic, less roasted. Some people prefer them. |
| **Single-origin nibs** | Chocolate shops, online | Genuinely different by origin — Madagascar is bright, Ecuador is floral. |
| **Honey-coated or candied nibs** | Specialty | Sweetened and much easier to like. Good on a family board. |
| **Nibs in dark chocolate bars** | Chocolate shops | Not the same ingredient, but a good way to get the texture. |

**Label check:** cacao beans and nothing else. Sugar in the ingredients means a
candied product, which is fine but different.

**Skip:** anything sold as cocoa powder or drinking chocolate. Different form,
different job.

## Quick FAQ

**What do cocoa nibs taste like?**
Intensely bitter, roasted and nutty. Like unsweetened dark chocolate with a crunch.
Not sweet at all.

**Can you eat cocoa nibs on their own?**
You can, but most people find them too bitter. On a board they belong scattered
over something sweet or creamy.

**Are cocoa nibs the same as cacao nibs?**
Same thing. Cacao usually implies less roasting and a fruitier, more acidic
flavour.

**Do they contain caffeine?**
A little, plus theobromine. Roughly comparable to dark chocolate by weight.

**How much per person?**
A quarter ounce. They are a scatter, not a serving.
