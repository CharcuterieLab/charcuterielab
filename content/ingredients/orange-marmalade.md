## The short version

Marmalade is the one preserve that's genuinely bitter, and bitterness is what a
rich board runs out of. A spoonful next to sharp cheddar or stilton does
something no fig jam can. Buy **Seville orange, thick-cut** — the fine-shred
sweet stuff is closer to jelly.

## What it is

Citrus preserve made with the peel as well as the flesh, which is where the
bitterness lives. **Seville** oranges are the traditional marmalade fruit — too
sour to eat raw, ideal cooked. Cut is graded from fine shred to thick cut,
meaning how coarsely the peel is sliced.

**Taste:** sweet, sharply acidic, distinctly bitter on the finish
**Texture:** set jelly suspending strips of peel
**Look:** amber to deep orange, translucent, shot through with peel

The mechanism is a three-part sequence: sugar, acid, then bitterness from the
pith. Nothing else on a board delivers all three, which is why marmalade can
follow a very rich mouthful and genuinely reset it. It's also why it divides
people — the bitterness is not incidental, it's the product.

## What it pairs with — and why

| Pair it with | Why it works |
|---|---|
| **Aged cheddar** | The British pairing. Sharp cheese, bitter-sweet peel — both are assertive and neither backs down. |
| **Stilton and blue cheese** | Bitterness handles the fat where sugar alone would only mask the salt. |
| **Fresh goat cheese** | Lactic tang plus citrus acid plus bitter peel. Three sharp things that stack rather than clash. |
| **Brie** | Acid and bitterness slicing soft fat; better than a sweet jam on a very ripe brie. |
| **Duck prosciutto** | Orange with duck is classical, and the bitterness cuts the fat. |
| **Dark chocolate** | The dessert corner. Orange and chocolate, unchanged for centuries. |

**Skip it with:** delicate fresh cheeses like burrata and mozzarella. Marmalade
will bury them.

## How to prep it for the board

1. **Decant into a small bowl** with its own spreader.
2. **Buy thick-cut.** The peel is the texture and the bitterness both.
3. **Serve 0.5 oz per person** — it's assertive, and people take less of it.
4. **Put it beside the cheddar or the blue.**
5. **Tell people it's marmalade.** Some will love it and some won't touch it, and
   both are fine.
6. **Warm it slightly** to loosen if it's very set.
7. **Fix:** too bitter for the room? A milder sweet-orange marmalade or a
   blood-orange version is gentler.

| **Frank Cooper's Oxford** | British delis, online | The benchmark. Dark and very bitter. |
| **Bonne Maman orange marmalade** | Any grocery | Sweeter and finer-cut. A gentler entry point. |
| **Blood orange marmalade** | Specialty, Italian delis | Less bitter, more berry. Excellent with goat cheese. |
| **Whisky or ginger marmalade** | British delis | Both work well with cheddar. |

**Label check:** "thick cut" or "coarse cut" on the front, Seville oranges in the
ingredients. Fine-shred sweet-orange marmalade is a much milder product.

**Skip:** orange jelly with no peel — that's just sugar.

## Quick FAQ

**What cheese goes with marmalade?**
Aged cheddar above all, plus stilton and blue cheeses, fresh goat cheese, and
brie.

**Why is marmalade bitter?**
It's made with the peel and pith, which carry bitter compounds. That bitterness
is the point.

**Seville or sweet orange marmalade?**
Seville for a cheese board — it's sharper and more bitter. Sweet orange versions
are much milder.

**What does thick cut mean?**
How coarsely the peel is shredded. Thick cut gives more texture and more
bitterness.

**How much per person?**
About half an ounce. It's assertive, so people use less of it than fig jam.
