## The short version

A marble-sized orange berry wrapped in a dry papery husk. Peel the husk back into
petals and leave it attached, and you have a fruit with a built-in handle that
looks like it came from a restaurant. They are tart, sweet and faintly savoury, and
three of them on a board do more visual work than a whole bowl of grapes.

## What it is

Physalis peruviana, a relative of the tomatillo. The berry ripens inside a lantern
of dry calyx, which protects it and keeps it for weeks. Sold in punnets, usually
imported from Colombia, Peru or South Africa.

**Taste:** tart and sweet at once, with a faint tomato note behind it
**Texture:** taut skin that bursts, with tiny soft seeds inside
**Look:** glossy amber-orange berry inside a straw-coloured paper husk

The mechanism is acid in a small package. The berry is high in citric and malic
acid for its size, so a single one delivers a sharp hit that a grape cannot — and
because it comes with its own handle, it can sit on a board for hours without being
touched, then still be picked up cleanly. It is one of very few fruits that solves
both the flavour and the handling problem at once.

## What it pairs with — and why

| Pair it with | Why it works |
|---|---|
| **Dark chocolate** | The pairing chocolatiers already make — physalis dipped in dark chocolate is a classic petit four. Tart fruit and bitterness. |
| **Brie** | Bloomy mild cheese with sharp fruit, and the husk looks extraordinary against a white rind. |
| **Mascarpone** | Sweet cream and tart berry. It needs nothing else. |
| **Triple-crème** | Very rich cheese needs sharp acid more than it needs sugar. |
| **Berries** | Same corner, different shapes and acid levels. Physalis gives the fruit corner a focal point. |
| **Honey** | Dip the berry halfway. Sweet against tart, and it looks like it was plated. |

**Skip it with:** cured meat and brined items. This is a fruit-corner and
dessert-corner ingredient.

## How to prep it for the board

1. **Peel the husk back** into four or five petals, but never tear it off.
2. **Twist the petals slightly** so they hold their shape.
3. **Wipe the berry** — there is a natural sticky film under the husk.
4. **Stand them upright** or lean them against a cheese. They are a focal point.
5. **Serve two or three per person.**
6. **Add them last.** The husk is fragile once opened.
7. **Fix:** husk brittle and shattering? They are old but the berry is usually still
   fine. Remove the husk entirely and use them like berries.

## What to buy

| Product | Where | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Physalis in a punnet** | Larger supermarkets, greengrocers | The common form. Check the berries feel firm through the husk. |
| **Goldenberries** | Health food shops | The same fruit under a different name. |
| **Chocolate-dipped physalis** | Chocolatiers, patisseries | Already the pairing. Excellent on a dessert board. |
| **Dried goldenberries** | Health food shops | Chewy and very tart. A good dried fruit, no visual effect. |
| **Ground cherries** | Farmers markets, late summer | The smaller sweeter North American cousin. Worth buying on sight. |

**Label check:** husks should be straw-coloured and dry, not green or damp. Berries
should feel firm.

**Skip:** punnets with any sign of mould inside the husks — it spreads fast.

## Quick FAQ

**What is physalis called?**
Cape gooseberry, goldenberry, ground cherry and Peruvian groundcherry all refer to
it. Physalis is the genus name and what most shops use.

**Do you eat the husk?**
No. Peel it back like petals and leave it attached — it is the handle and the whole
visual effect.

**What does it taste like?**
Sharply sweet and tart, with a tomato-like note underneath. Closer to a tart
pineapple than to a berry.

**Is it related to the tomatillo?**
Yes, closely. Tomatillos are the savoury member of the same family and grow in the
same papery husk.

**How much per person?**
Two or three berries. They are a garnish and a punctuation mark.
