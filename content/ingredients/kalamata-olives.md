## The short version

The dark olive most people picture. Kalamatas are almond-shaped, deep purple-
black, and cured in brine and red wine vinegar, which gives them a fruity, winey
sharpness quite different from a plain black olive. Cheap, everywhere, and
genuinely good.

## What it is

A Greek variety grown around Kalamata in the Peloponnese, harvested ripe and
brine-cured, usually with red wine vinegar. Firm and meaty, with a pit. The
canned "black olives" in American supermarkets are a completely different thing —
lye-cured, oxidised, and mild to the point of blandness.

**Taste:** salty, winey, fruity, moderately bitter
**Texture:** meaty and firm, with a slight chew
**Look:** deep purple-black, almond-shaped, glossy

The mechanism is acid on top of brine. The wine vinegar in the cure adds a
sharpness that a plain salt brine doesn't, which is why Kalamatas cut fat better
than most olives. That makes them the right olive for a board with a lot of cheese
and a lot of oil.

## What it pairs with — and why

| Pair it with | Why it works |
|---|---|
| **Feta** | The Greek pairing. Both salty, but feta is soft and lactic while the olive is meaty and winey — the contrast is texture as much as flavour. |
| **Manchego and hard sheep cheeses** | Salt and nuttiness against fruity brine. |
| **Hummus and mezze dips** | Brine cutting through tahini's fat, on the same plate they come from. |
| **Aged cheddar** | Sharp against sharp; the vinegar in the cure meets the cheese's acidity. |
| **Pita chips and flatbread** | The base for everything above. |
| **Tapenade** | They're the main ingredient. Serving both is doubling down, which works on a Mediterranean board. |

**Skip it with:** burrata, fresh mozzarella and delicate bloomy cheeses.
Kalamatas will flatten all three.

## How to prep it for the board

1. **Drain and pat dry**, then dress lightly with olive oil if they look dull.
2. **Serve in a small bowl** with a pit bowl beside it.
3. **Buy them with pits.** Pitted Kalamatas are mushier and take on more brine.
4. **Serve 1 oz per person.**
5. **Warm them with orange zest, garlic and rosemary** for five minutes if you
   want to make an effort — it transforms them.
6. **Serve at room temperature.** Cold olives taste of much less.
7. **Fix:** too salty? Rinse briefly and re-dress with olive oil and lemon.

## What to buy

| Product | Where | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Kalamata olives in brine** | Olive bar, any grocery | The default. Olive bar is fresher than jarred. |
| **Divina or Mt. Vikos Kalamata** | Grocery, specialty | Reliable jarred brands. |
| **Greek deli Kalamata** | Greek and Middle Eastern grocers | Often the best and cheapest. |
| **Pitted Kalamata** | Any grocery | Convenient, softer, saltier. Fine for cooking, second-best on a board. |
| **Kalamata in olive oil** | Specialty | Richer and less sharp. Good with fresh cheese. |

**Label check:** "Kalamata" should be the variety, and the cure should include
vinegar. PDO Kalamata from Greece is the real thing.

**Skip:** canned California black olives. Different product, and much blander.

## Quick FAQ

**What's the difference between Kalamata and black olives?**
Kalamatas are a Greek variety brine-cured with wine vinegar — winey and sharp.
Canned black olives are lye-cured and oxidised, and much milder.

**Are Kalamata olives salty?**
Yes, noticeably. Rinse them briefly and dress with olive oil if they're too much.

**Pitted or with pits?**
With pits. Pitted Kalamatas absorb more brine and go soft.

**What cheese goes with Kalamata olives?**
Feta above all, plus manchego, hard sheep cheeses, and aged cheddar.

**How much per person?**
About an ounce — four or five olives.
