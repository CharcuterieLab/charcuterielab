## The short version

Dry-cured, often smoked ham from the American upland South — Virginia,
Kentucky, Tennessee, Missouri. Considerably saltier and funkier than European
hams, and the long-aged versions from the best producers are as good as
anything made anywhere.

## What it is

Country ham is salt-cured, often with sugar and pepper, then smoked over
hickory and aged from a few months to well over a year. The tradition predates
refrigeration and was built for keeping through hot Southern summers, which is
why the salt level is high. Benton's, Edwards and Col. Bill Newsom's are the
names that matter.

**Taste:** intensely salty, deeply savoury, smoky, funky and complex when aged
long
**Texture:** firm and dry, chewier than prosciutto
**Look:** dark mahogany-red, sometimes with a white surface bloom

Aggressive salt over long aging is the mechanism. Higher salt than any European
dry-cure was necessary in a hot climate — and it also slows enzymatic breakdown,
so a country ham needs longer to develop complexity. Aged eighteen months or
more, that combination produces a funk and depth prosciutto never reaches.

## What it pairs with — and why

| Pair it with | Why it works |
|---|---|
| **Buttermilk biscuits** | The Southern service, and the correct one. Soft, fatty, unsalted bread against a very salty ham. |
| **Oatcakes** | Sweet oat against hard salt. A better cracker for this than a plain one. |
| **Aged cheddar** | Sharp against salty. Works, though it's a lot of intensity. |
| **Maytag Blue or a Southern blue** | Salt and funk on both sides. For a serious board. |
| **Melon, peach or fig** | Sweet cold fruit is essential relief. |
| **Sorghum syrup or honey** | Sweetness against salt. Sorghum is the regional answer. |
| **Bourbon, cider, sparkling wine** | Sweetness or bubbles cutting salt. |

**Skip it with:** smoked gouda and other smoked items, and delicate fresh
cheeses. Country ham is the saltiest meat in this section and it takes over.

## How to prep it for the board

1. **Slice paper-thin.** Thicker than that and the salt is punishing. This is
   the single most important step.
2. **Serve three-quarters of an ounce to an ounce per person.** Less than any
   European ham.
3. **Put something sweet directly beside it** — melon, fig, sorghum, honey.
   Without it, one slice is enough for most people.
4. **Temper 20 minutes.**
5. **Drape rather than stack.**
6. **Try it warmed too.** A slice crisped for a minute in a dry pan is a
   different and excellent thing.
7. **Fix:** too salty for the room? Serve it on a biscuit with butter rather
   than plain. Fat and starch blunt salt better than anything else.

| **Jamón serrano** | Most grocery | The Spanish equivalent, milder — see its own page. |
| **La Quercia Prosciutto Americano** | Whole Foods | American, but Italian-style and much gentler. |

**Label check:** an aging time. Long-aged (12+ months) hams are for slicing
thin and eating raw; short-cured supermarket slices are meant to be fried.

**Skip:** eating a short-cured supermarket country ham slice raw. Those are
cooking hams and they're punishingly salty uncooked.

## Quick FAQ

**Can you eat country ham raw?**
Long-aged ones, yes — they're dry-cured like prosciutto. Short-cured supermarket
slices are meant to be fried.

**Why is it so salty?**
It was developed to survive hot Southern summers without refrigeration. High
salt was the whole preservation strategy.

**How is it different from prosciutto?**
Saltier, usually smoked, and often funkier. The best long-aged versions are
comparable in quality but very different in character.

**How much per person?**
Under an ounce, sliced very thin.

**What's the classic pairing?**
A buttermilk biscuit. Fat and soft starch are what make the salt work.
