## The short version

Twice-baked Italian biscuits, dry and hard enough that they're meant to be dipped
— traditionally in vin santo, on a board in mascarpone or a sweet wine. They're
the one thing in the dessert corner substantial enough to feel like a course
rather than a nibble.

## What it is

A stiff dough baked in a log, sliced, then baked again to dry out. Tuscan
*cantucci* are the almond original; modern versions come with pistachio,
chocolate, cranberry, anise or chocolate-dipped ends. The double bake is what
makes them hard and shelf-stable for weeks.

**Taste:** sweet, nutty, faintly of almond extract or anise
**Texture:** very hard and dry, softening when dipped
**Look:** long angled slices with visible whole nuts

The mechanism is porosity. Two bakes drive out nearly all the moisture, leaving a
biscuit that's structurally a sponge — it absorbs whatever it's dipped in without
falling apart. That makes it the only sweet base on a board that can carry a wet
topping the way a cracker carries cheese.

## What it pairs with — and why

| Pair it with | Why it works |
|---|---|
| **Mascarpone** | Dip the biscotti in it. Hard and dry against soft and rich — the reason both are on the board. |
| **Dark chocolate** | Same corner, and chocolate-dipped biscotti is a product for a reason. |
| **Brandied cherries** | Spoon the syrup over a biscotti. Boozy, sweet, and it softens the biscuit exactly right. |
| **Fresh ricotta with honey** | Scoop it. The biscotti is the vehicle. |
| **Gorgonzola** | Unexpected and good — sweet nutty biscuit against salt and funk, the same logic as honey. |
| **Vin santo, dessert wine, espresso** | The traditional service, and the point of the texture. |

**Skip it with:** savoury cheese and cured meat. Biscotti are sweet and belong at
the end.

## How to prep it for the board

1. **Stand them in a jar or glass**, or fan them at the edge of the board. They're
   long and awkward laid flat.
2. **Break long ones in half** so they're a manageable size.
3. **Put something to dip into next to them** — mascarpone, ricotta, or a small
   glass of dessert wine.
4. **Serve 0.75 oz per person** — one biscotti each.
5. **Buy or make them small.** Restaurant-sized biscotti are too much.
6. **Keep them sealed.** They soften in humidity, which defeats the purpose.
7. **Fix:** too hard to bite? That's correct. Provide something to dip in.

**Label check:** traditional cantucci contain no butter or oil, which is why
they're so hard. Softer supermarket versions add fat.

**Skip:** anything individually wrapped — the packaging on a board looks wrong.

## Quick FAQ

**Why is biscotti so hard?**
It's baked twice to drive out moisture. The hardness is deliberate — it's designed
to be dipped.

**What do you dip biscotti in?**
Traditionally vin santo. On a board, mascarpone, fresh ricotta, coffee, or the
syrup from brandied cherries.

**What's the difference between biscotti and cantucci?**
Cantucci are the traditional Tuscan almond version, made without butter. Biscotti
is the broader term, and modern versions are often softer.

**Do they go with cheese?**
With sweet cheeses like mascarpone and ricotta, yes — and surprisingly well with
gorgonzola.

**How long do they keep?**
Weeks in a sealed container. They're designed to be shelf-stable.
