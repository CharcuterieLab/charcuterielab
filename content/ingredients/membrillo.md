## The short version

Quince cooked with sugar until it sets into a firm, sliceable block — a jam you
cut with a knife rather than spread. *Membrillo y manchego* is the Spanish
pairing, and it's on this list because it's a jam that needs no bowl, no
spreader, and no explanation.

## What it is

Quince paste, sold as a rectangular block or a small round. Spanish membrillo and
Portuguese *marmelada* are the same thing; Latin American *ate de membrillo* is
close. The fruit is inedible raw — hard, astringent, sour — and only becomes this
after hours of cooking, which turns it deep amber.

**Taste:** sweet, floral, faintly of apple and rose, mildly tannic
**Texture:** firm and dense, like a very stiff jelly; slices cleanly
**Look:** translucent amber-red batons or slices

The mechanism is jam in solid form. Every other sweet element on a board needs a
vessel and a utensil, and guests have to assemble the bite themselves. Membrillo
is pre-portioned — a slice sits on a slice of cheese with no equipment — which is
why it appears on almost every Spanish cheese plate.

## What it pairs with — and why

| Pair it with | Why it works |
|---|---|
| **Manchego** | The pairing. Salt and sheep-milk fat against floral sugar, with both firm enough to eat stacked in the hand. |
| **Idiazábal and aged sheep cheeses** | Same logic with smoke added; the quince's floral edge cuts through it. |
| **Marcona almonds** | The full Spanish plate: cheese, quince, almond. Three textures, one region. |
| **Jamón serrano** | Salt and fat against dense sugar, with the meat's chew matched by the paste's. |
| **Aged gouda and hard cheeses** | Anything crystalline and salty pairs the same way manchego does. |
| **Blue cheese** | Firm sweetness handling salt, without the mess of a spoonable jam. |

**Skip it with:** soft fresh cheeses. Membrillo is too dense to eat with
something that has no structure.

## How to prep it for the board

1. **Cut it into batons or thin slices**, about the size of the cheese slices
   they'll sit on.
2. **Use a hot dry knife** if it sticks — wipe between cuts.
3. **Lay the slices directly against the manchego.** Stacking them shows people
   what to do.
4. **Or cut small cubes** and skewer them with cheese on picks.
5. **Serve 0.5 oz per person.**
6. **Keep the block wrapped** in the fridge; it lasts months.
7. **Fix:** sweating or sticky? Blot with paper towel and let it come to room
   temperature before slicing.

## What to buy

| Product | Where | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Spanish membrillo** | Whole Foods, cheese shops, Spanish delis | The standard. Sold in a small block or round. |
| **Mitica or Fermin membrillo** | Cheese shops, online | Better quality, deeper flavour. Worth it. |
| **Portuguese marmelada** | Portuguese and Brazilian markets | Same product, often cheaper. |
| **Ate de membrillo** | Latin markets | Latin American version. Sometimes sweeter. |
| **Guava paste (ate de guayaba)** | Latin markets | Not quince, but the same idea and excellent with salty cheese. |

**Label check:** quince and sugar should be the only real ingredients. Some
cheaper versions bulk it out with apple.

**Skip:** anything with added pectin listed high — it sets rubbery rather than
dense.

## Quick FAQ

**What is membrillo?**
Quince paste — quince cooked with sugar until it sets firm enough to slice. A
Spanish cheese-board staple.

**What cheese goes with membrillo?**
Manchego above all, plus other aged sheep cheeses, aged gouda, and blue cheese.

**How do I cut membrillo?**
Into thin slices or batons with a hot dry knife, wiped between cuts.

**Is membrillo the same as quince jelly?**
No — jelly is spoonable, membrillo is firm enough to slice and eat by hand.

**How long does it keep?**
Months in the fridge, wrapped. It's a preserve, and a very stable one.
