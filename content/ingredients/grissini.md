## The short version

Long, thin, brittle Italian breadsticks. They taste mild and mostly of olive
oil, and you are not really buying them for flavor — you're buying the only
ingredient that makes a board taller than an inch. Wrap them in prosciutto and
they become the thing people photograph.

## What it is

Grissini come from Turin, where they've been made since the 1600s. Two styles
still exist: *rubatà*, hand-rolled and knobbly and irregular, and *stirati*,
stretched straight and uniform. The rubatà look better on a board; the stirati
are what most supermarkets stock.

**Taste:** mild, wheaty, olive-oil savory
**Texture:** brittle, dry, snaps loudly
**Look:** long and pale gold, either knobbly or pencil-straight

Height is the mechanism, and it's a visual one rather than a flavor one. Every
other item on a board lies flat. Grissini break the plane, and a board with one
vertical element reads as composed instead of arranged.

## What it pairs with — and why

| Pair it with | Why it works |
|---|---|
| **Prosciutto, wrapped** | The iconic move. Salt and silk spiraled around dry crunch, and it turns one ingredient into a finished bite. |
| **Mortadella and coppa** | Same technique, softer and sweeter. Mortadella wraps more easily than prosciutto. |
| **Burrata and stracchino** | The stick is a handle. Soft cheese has nothing to hold it otherwise. |
| **Aged parmesan** | Snap a stick, snap off a shard of parmesan. Dry against dry, salt against wheat. |
| **Olive oil and aged balsamic** | Dip the end. The most Italian thing on the board and it costs nothing. |
| **Aperol spritz, prosecco, dry vermouth** | This is *aperitivo* food. Bitter and bubbly against plain salted bread. |
| **Marinated olives and artichokes** | Oily antipasti next to something dry that isn't a cracker. |

**Skip it with:** thick dips — they snap off in the bowl. And heavy spreads,
which are more than the stick can carry.

## How to prep it for the board

1. **Stand them upright in a heavy glass, small vase or jar** at one corner of
   the board. A tipped-over glass ruins the effect, so use something with
   weight.
2. **Wrap the prosciutto right before serving.** The meat's moisture softens
   the stick within about an hour, and a soft grissino is a sad thing.
3. **Wrap technique:** tear a slice of prosciutto lengthwise in half, start at
   one end and spiral loosely down two-thirds of the stick. Leave the top bare
   so there's a handle.
4. **Serve 2–3 per person**, or 1 wrapped plus 1 plain.
5. **Snap them in half for a small board.** Full-length grissini overwhelm
   anything under about 12 inches.
6. **Fix:** softened sticks come back at 300°F for 4 minutes, laid flat on a
   sheet. Cool completely before standing them up.

**Label check:** extra virgin olive oil in the ingredients, not palm or
vegetable oil. It's most of what you taste.

**Skip:** soft garlic breadsticks from the bakery case. Different food
entirely — those are a side dish, not a board ingredient.

## Quick FAQ

**How do I wrap grissini in prosciutto?**
Halve a slice lengthwise, spiral it loosely down two-thirds of the stick, leave
the top bare as a handle. Do it within an hour of serving.

**How many breadsticks per person?**
2–3, or one wrapped and one plain.

**Rubatà or stirati — does it matter?**
For taste, barely. For photographs, a lot. The knobbly hand-rolled kind looks
handmade because it is.

**Can I make them ahead?**
Wrapped, no — an hour at most. Plain, they keep for weeks in a sealed bag.

**Why did mine go soft on the board?**
Moisture, usually from the prosciutto or from sitting next to cut fruit. Wrap
late and place them away from anything wet.
