## The short version

The most practical fresh fruit on a board. Halve one, twist out the pit, set it
cut-side up: it holds its shape, doesn't leak, barely browns, and makes a small
orange cup you can drop a cheese crumble or a pistachio into. Season is short —
late May through July.

## What it is

A small stone fruit, velvety-skinned, more acidic than a peach and much less
juicy. Ripe apricots are soft to the thumb and smell honeyed; unripe ones are
firm, sour, and mealy, which is how most supermarket apricots are sold.

**Taste:** tart-sweet, faintly almond
**Texture:** dense, dry-fleshed, barely juicy
**Look:** orange-gold halves with a shallow hollow

The mechanism is acid without water. A grape resets the palate by rinsing it; an
apricot does it by cutting through fat with sharpness. That makes it a better
partner for very rich cheeses than a sweeter, wetter fruit would be, and it's why
apricot and goat cheese has been a fixed pairing for a century.

## What it pairs with — and why

| Pair it with | Why it works |
|---|---|
| **Fresh goat cheese** | Two acids that don't compete — the fruit's is fruity, the cheese's is lactic. The hollow holds a crumble of it. |
| **Manchego** | Sheep-milk nuttiness meets the apricot's almond note; they share the same aromatic family. |
| **Prosciutto** | Tart and salty, with none of the mess a peach makes. |
| **Pistachios** | The classic pairing in Middle Eastern cooking, and it works cold. Green against orange. |
| **Honey drizzled over** | Fills in the sugar an underripe apricot is missing. |
| **Brie** | Acid cutting soft fat; the halves sit neatly against a wedge. |

**Skip it with:** sweet jams and chutneys. Apricot is already the sweet-tart
element — doubling it flattens the board.

## How to prep it for the board

1. **Buy them soft.** Firm apricots are mealy and never improve much.
2. **Ripen on the counter** in a paper bag for a day or two if they're firm.
3. **Halve along the natural seam** and twist. The pit lifts straight out.
4. **Leave the skin on** — it's thin and holds the half together.
5. **Set them cut-side up.** They function as little bowls, and they don't
   brown much this way.
6. **Serve 1–2 oz per person** — one apricot serves two.
7. **Fix:** underripe and sour? Halve, drizzle with honey, and broil for three
   minutes. Or use dried apricots instead — they're better than bad fresh ones.

## What to buy

| Product | Where | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Fresh apricots** | Grocery, farmers markets, May–Jul | Buy soft and fragrant, not firm. |
| **Blenheim apricots** | California farmers markets, June | The great one. Intensely aromatic, worth seeking out. |
| **Apriums / pluots** | Grocery, summer | Apricot-plum crosses. Sweeter, juicier, work the same way. |
| **Dried apricots** | Any grocery, year-round | The reliable substitute for ten months of the year. |

**Label check:** smell them. A ripe apricot is strongly fragrant at the stem;
a silent one will taste like nothing.

**Skip:** hard pale-orange apricots and anything with wrinkled skin at the stem.

## Quick FAQ

**When are apricots in season?**
Late May through July. It's a short window, and out-of-season apricots aren't
worth buying.

**Do apricots brown on a board?**
Much less than peaches or apples. Cut-side up, they'll hold for a couple of
hours.

**Do I peel them?**
No. The skin is thin and keeps the half intact.

**What if mine are hard and sour?**
Ripen in a paper bag for a day, or halve and broil with honey. Otherwise use
dried apricots.

**How many per person?**
One apricot serves two people on a mixed board.
