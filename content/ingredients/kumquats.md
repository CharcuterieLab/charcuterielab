## The short version

A citrus with the sugar in the skin and the acid in the flesh, so you eat it
whole and it flips halfway through. Nothing else on a board does that. Small,
bright orange, and cheap enough to be a conversation piece rather than a splurge.

## What it is

A thumb-sized citrus, oval or round, eaten peel and all. **Nagami** is the oval
one and the one you'll find — tart flesh, sweet peel. **Meiwa** is round, sweeter
overall, harder to source. Season runs December through April.

**Taste:** sweet peel, sharply sour flesh, floral finish
**Texture:** firm skin, juicy interior, a couple of small seeds
**Look:** tiny glossy orange ovals, often sold on the branch with leaves

The mechanism is inversion. Every other citrus asks you to discard the bitter
part and eat the sweet part; a kumquat reverses it. On a board that's mostly
salt and fat, an ingredient that changes character mid-bite is genuinely useful —
it resets the palate twice.

## What it pairs with — and why

| Pair it with | Why it works |
|---|---|
| **Blue cheese** | Sweet peel handles the salt, sour flesh cuts the fat. The one citrus that works with blue. |
| **Fresh goat cheese** | Sharp against sharp, with the peel's sugar arriving after. |
| **Aged gouda** | Caramel and butterscotch against bright bitter citrus. |
| **Prosciutto** | Wrap a slice around a halved kumquat — salt, fat, sugar, acid in one bite. |
| **Almonds** | Bitter almond and bitter pith are the same register. |
| **Dark chocolate** | Candied-orange logic, without candying anything. |

**Skip it with:** delicate fresh cheeses. Kumquat is loud and will flatten
mozzarella or burrata.

## How to prep it for the board

1. **Wash well.** You're eating the peel, so it matters more than with other citrus.
2. **Roll each one firmly between your fingers** before serving. It breaks the
   oil cells in the skin and releases the aroma.
3. **Slice crosswise into thin wheels**, or halve them, or leave whole — all
   three are legitimate.
4. **Pick out the seeds** if you've sliced them.
5. **Buy them on the branch** if you can find them. Leaves and stems on a board
   look better than anything you can arrange.
6. **Serve 0.5–1 oz per person.** They're strong; nobody eats six.
7. **Fix:** too sour for your crowd? Halve, toss with a little honey, and let
   them sit twenty minutes. Or simmer whole in sugar syrup for ten minutes.

## What to buy

| Product | Where | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Nagami kumquats** | Grocery, Asian markets, Dec–Apr | The standard oval kumquat. Tart flesh, sweet peel. |
| **Meiwa kumquats** | Farmers markets, specialty | Round and sweeter throughout. Better for eating whole. |
| **Kumquats on the branch** | Asian markets, farmers markets | Worth paying extra for. The leaves make the board. |
| **Candied kumquats** | Specialty, Italian delis | Sweet enough to sit in the dessert corner beside chocolate. |

**Label check:** firm, glossy, deep orange. Soft or dull skin means old fruit,
and since you're eating the skin there's nowhere to hide.

**Skip:** pale yellow-orange kumquats — underripe, and the peel will be bitter
rather than sweet.

## Quick FAQ

**Do you eat kumquat skin?**
Yes — the skin is the sweet part. The flesh inside is the sour part.

**How do I serve kumquats on a board?**
Sliced into thin wheels, halved, or whole. Whole on the branch looks best.

**Are kumquats too sour?**
The flesh is sharp. Roll them first to release the peel oils, and pair them with
something salty like blue cheese or prosciutto.

**When are kumquats in season?**
December through April.

**Do they have seeds?**
Usually two or three small ones. Pick them out if you're slicing.
