## The short version

A coarse country terrine of pork, pork liver and fat, baked in a loaf and
served in slices. Rustic, peppery and deeply savoury. With a baguette, butter
and cornichons it's an entire French charcuterie plate for very little money.

## What it is

*Pâté de campagne* means country pâté. Coarsely chopped pork shoulder, belly
and liver are mixed with onion, garlic, pepper, sometimes brandy or wine, packed
into a terrine and baked. It's often wrapped in caul fat or lined with bacon.
Unlike a smooth mousse, the texture is deliberately rough.

**Taste:** savoury, peppery, mineral from the liver, gently sweet from onion
**Texture:** coarse and crumbly, firm enough to slice
**Look:** grey-pink flecked loaf, often with a darker bacon edge or a bay leaf
on top

Liver proportion is the mechanism. Enough liver gives the mineral depth that
makes it taste like pâté rather than meatloaf; too much and it turns bitter and
pasty. Country pâté sits at a modest ratio — usually a fifth to a third — which
is why it reads as savoury and rustic rather than rich and livery.

## What it pairs with — and why

| Pair it with | Why it works |
|---|---|
| **Cornichons** | Sharp vinegar against fat and liver. The single most important partner. |
| **Grainy or Dijon mustard** | Same job, more body. |
| **Baguette and butter** | The French plate. Bread carries it; butter softens the edge. |
| **Melba toast** | Dry and neutral under something rich and soft. |
| **Pickled onions and caperberries** | More acid, more crunch. |
| **Fig jam or cherry preserve** | Sweetness against mineral liver. Excellent and less obvious. |
| **Beaujolais, dry cider, Riesling** | Light, acidic, French. |

**Skip it with:** smoked cheese, which fights the liver. And skip serving it
next to another liver product — one pâté per board.

## How to prep it for the board

1. **Slice about ⅓ inch thick** and lay the slices flat. Thinner and they
   crumble apart.
2. **Or serve the terrine whole with a knife** and let people cut their own.
   It looks generous and it's how a French charcuterie does it.
3. **Temper 20 minutes.** Cold pâté is firm and mute.
4. **Serve 1–1.5 oz per person.**
5. **Cornichons and mustard directly beside it** — this is a pairing the dish
   depends on.
6. **Leave the bay leaf or bacon edge on** for looks; nobody eats it.
7. **Fix:** crumbling badly? Chill it briefly, then use a thin knife dipped in
   hot water between slices.

**Label check:** pork and pork liver near the top, a coarse visible texture, and
a short ingredient list. Smooth uniform pâté is a mousse, which is a different
product.

**Skip:** pâté in a sealed tin for a board if a fresh terrine is available. The
texture difference is large.

## Quick FAQ

**Is pâté de campagne the same as liver pâté?**
It contains liver but isn't dominated by it. Coarse pork is the bulk; liver is
maybe a fifth to a third.

**Is it cooked?**
Yes — baked in a terrine mould, then chilled. It's ready to eat.

**How is it different from rillettes?**
Pâté is ground and baked into a sliceable loaf. Rillettes are slow-cooked
shredded meat packed in fat, and they spread.

**How much per person?**
1–1.5 oz. It's rich.

**Do I need the cornichons?**
Effectively yes. Acid is what keeps a fatty liver terrine from feeling heavy.
