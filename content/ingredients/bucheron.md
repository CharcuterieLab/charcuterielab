## The short version

A French goat's-milk log aged five to ten weeks, with a bloomy white rind. Cut
a slice and you get a soft, almost liquid ring just under the rind and a firm,
chalky core in the middle. Two textures, one cheese, and it's excellent value.

## What it is

Bucheron — *bûcheron* means woodcutter — is a *bûche*, or log, of pasteurized
goat's milk cheese ripened with a white bloomy mold. The mold works from the
outside in, so the outer paste breaks down first while the center stays firm.
Cheesemongers call the visible boundary the *creamline*.

**Taste:** tangy and lemony at the core, richer and more mushroomy at the edge
**Texture:** runny ring under the rind, chalky and dense in the center
**Look:** white log with a bloomy rind and a visible creamline in cross-section

Directional ripening is the mechanism. Surface mold produces enzymes that break
down proteins and fats from the outside inward, and in a log of this diameter
the process reaches equilibrium before it finishes. The result is a cheese that
is genuinely two things at once, which is rare and worth pointing out at the
table.

## What it pairs with — and why

| Pair it with | Why it works |
|---|---|
| **Honey** | Sweet against goat's-milk acid. The core pairing for any chèvre. |
| **Seeded crackers** | Bitter seed oil and crunch against tang and chalk. |
| **Fig jam and cherry preserve** | Dense fruit sweetness with a bright, acidic cheese. |
| **Walnuts and pistachios** | Tannin and crunch, and pistachio green against white paste looks good. |
| **Beets** | Earthy and sweet against sharp — the salad, taken apart. |
| **Fresh thyme** | Goat cheese takes herbs better than any other family. |
| **Sancerre, Sauvignon Blanc, dry rosé** | High-acid Loire whites. The regional pairing. |

**Skip it with:** blue cheese, which flattens it, and everything crackers,
where garlic fights the tang.

## How to prep it for the board

1. **Cut discs with dental floss or a wire**, not a knife. A blade drags the
   soft outer ring into the chalky center and ruins the cross-section.
2. **Lay the discs flat so the creamline shows.** That ring is the reason to
   buy this cheese rather than a plain chèvre.
3. **Temper 30 minutes** — the outer ring should be visibly soft.
4. **Serve 1–1.5 oz per person.**
5. **Point out the two textures.** People assume it's one cheese ripening
   unevenly; telling them it's deliberate changes how they eat it.
6. **Eat the rind or trim it** — it's edible and mild.
7. **Fix:** if the whole log is chalky through, it's young. A few more days in
   the fridge will widen the creamline.

| **Fresh chèvre** | Any grocery | The young, unaged version — see its own page. |

**Label check:** buy a piece cut from the log so you can see the creamline. A
wide soft ring means a well-ripened cheese; an all-chalk cross-section means
it's young.

**Skip:** pre-wrapped whole logs if the counter will cut one. You want to see
the cross-section before you buy.

## Quick FAQ

**Why is the middle chalky and the outside runny?**
The bloomy rind ripens the cheese from the outside inward. In a log this thick,
the center hasn't broken down yet — and that contrast is the point.

**Is the chalky part underripe?**
No. It's characteristic of the style and it's supposed to be there.

**How do I slice it cleanly?**
Dental floss or thin wire. A knife drags the soft ring into the core.

**Can you eat the rind?**
Yes. It's thin and mild.

**How much per person?**
1–1.5 oz.
