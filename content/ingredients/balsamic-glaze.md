## The short version

Balsamic vinegar reduced until it's syrupy — sweet, sharp and thick enough to
drizzle in a line that stays where you put it. On a board it does what a jam does
without needing a bowl, a spoon, or anyone's attention. Ten minutes on the stove
makes it from a cheap bottle.

## What it is

Balsamic vinegar cooked down with or without added sugar until it coats a spoon.
Sold as *glassa*, glaze, or crema di balsamico. The real thing — aged
*aceto balsamico tradizionale* — is naturally syrupy and costs a hundred dollars a
bottle; a glaze is the affordable stand-in.

**Taste:** sweet, sharply acidic, faintly woody
**Texture:** thick and syrupy, coats
**Look:** near-black, glossy

The mechanism is reduction. Boiling off water concentrates both the sugar and the
acetic acid, so a glaze delivers a bigger hit of each than the vinegar it came
from — and the viscosity keeps it sitting on the surface where you taste it,
rather than running off into the board.

## What it pairs with — and why

| Pair it with | Why it works |
|---|---|
| **Burrata and fresh mozzarella** | Sweet acid cutting cream, in the two seconds it takes to drizzle. The default finish. |
| **Berries and strawberries** | Balsamic on strawberries is an Italian classic — the acid makes the fruit taste sweeter. |
| **Parmigiano-Reggiano** | The traditional pairing. Salt, crystals and umami against sweet acid. |
| **Prosciutto and melon** | Adds the acid that the salt-and-sweet combination is missing. |
| **Roasted vegetables and tomatoes** | Standard, and it works cold on a board. |
| **Fresh figs** | Halved figs with balsamic and blue cheese is a complete dish. |

**Skip it with:** delicate aged cheeses you want to taste, and anything already
sweet and sticky.

## How to prep it for the board

1. **Drizzle in a zigzag** over cheese or fruit, right before serving.
2. **Or pour a small pool** beside bread and oil.
3. **Make it:** simmer 1 cup of ordinary balsamic vinegar over medium-low heat for
   10–15 minutes until it coats a spoon. It thickens more as it cools.
4. **Use a squeeze bottle** if you have one — the line is cleaner.
5. **Add it last.** It bleeds into soft cheese if it sits.
6. **Don't over-drizzle.** It stains and it's sweeter than it looks.
7. **Fix:** too thin? Reduce further. Too thick? A teaspoon of water or vinegar.

## What to buy

| Product | Where | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Glassa di balsamico** | Italian delis, grocery | The standard glaze. Sweet and thick. |
| **Bertolli or Colavita glaze** | Any grocery | Widely available and fine. |
| **Homemade reduction** | Make it | Ten minutes with a cheap bottle. Less sweet and better. |
| **Aceto balsamico tradizionale di Modena DOP** | Specialty, very expensive | The real thing, aged 12–25 years. Naturally syrupy, extraordinary, and used by the drop. |
| **Fig or cherry balsamic glaze** | Specialty | Flavoured versions. Good with cheese, sometimes over-sweet. |

**Label check:** on glazes, check where sugar sits in the ingredients — a lot of
them are more sugar than vinegar. On vinegar, "Aceto Balsamico di Modena IGP" is
the reliable everyday grade.

**Skip:** anything labelled "balsamic dressing" — that's a vinaigrette.

## Quick FAQ

**What is balsamic glaze?**
Balsamic vinegar reduced to a syrup, sometimes with added sugar. Thick enough to
drizzle and stay put.

**Can I make it myself?**
Yes. Simmer a cup of ordinary balsamic for 10–15 minutes until it coats a spoon.
It thickens further as it cools.

**What do you drizzle it on?**
Burrata, fresh mozzarella, berries, Parmigiano-Reggiano, and prosciutto with
melon.

**Is glaze the same as traditional balsamic?**
No. Traditional aceto balsamico is aged for over a decade and is naturally
syrupy. A glaze is a reduction, often sweetened, at a fraction of the price.

**Does it contain allergens?**
Sulphites, as with most vinegars — worth noting for sensitive guests.
