## The short version

A Tuscan sheep's-milk cheese aged twenty days to six months. Mild, buttery and
faintly sweet — genuinely nothing like the hard, salty Pecorino Romano most
Americans know. If you decided you don't like pecorino, you probably met the
wrong one.

## What it is

Pecorino Toscano DOP is made across Tuscany from sheep's milk, in two grades:
*fresco* (aged 20 days minimum, soft and mild) and *stagionato* (aged four
months or more, firmer and nuttier). Unlike Romano, it isn't heavily
dry-salted — the salting is light and the aging short.

**Taste:** buttery, nutty, faintly sweet, mild sheep tang
**Texture:** semi-soft and supple when young; firm and slightly crumbly aged
**Look:** ivory to pale straw paste, rind ranging from pale yellow to deep red
or black

Salt handling is the mechanism, and it's the entire difference from Romano.
Both are sheep's-milk cheeses from Italy; Romano is dry-salted repeatedly over
weeks, Toscano is brined briefly. Same milk, opposite results — one is a
seasoning, the other is a table cheese.

## What it pairs with — and why

| Pair it with | Why it works |
|---|---|
| **Fresh pear and apple** | Sweet water against buttery sheep's milk. The Tuscan default. |
| **Honey — especially chestnut** | The traditional Italian sheep-cheese pairing. |
| **Fava beans, in spring** | Fresh, green and sweet against mild salt. |
| **Prosciutto and finocchiona** | Tuscan cured pork with Tuscan cheese. |
| **Taralli and grissini** | Plain Italian bread; nothing complicated needed. |
| **Walnuts** | Tannin and oil against a buttery paste. |
| **Chianti, Vernaccia, Sangiovese** | Tuscan wines, medium-bodied. |

**Skip it with:** delicate fresh cheeses in the same corner, and Pecorino
Romano on the same board — the comparison makes Toscano taste bland even though
it isn't.

## How to prep it for the board

1. **Buy *stagionato* for a board.** The young *fresco* is pleasant but soft
   and unremarkable; the aged version has the nuttiness worth serving.
2. **Cut into thin wedges** with the rind on the outer edge.
3. **Temper 30 minutes.**
4. **Serve 1–2 oz per person.**
5. **Put honey and pear beside it.** This cheese is mild enough that it wants a
   partner rather than working alone.
6. **Trim the rind** — some are rubbed with tomato or ash and none are edible.
7. **Fix:** flat and rubbery? That's *fresco*, and it's meant to be. Serve it
   with something sweet or buy the aged grade.

## What to buy

| Product | Where | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Pecorino Toscano DOP, *stagionato*** | Cheese counters, Whole Foods, Italian delis | The aged grade. The one to buy for a board. |
| **Pecorino Toscano DOP, *fresco*** | Cheese counters | Young and soft. Pleasant, unremarkable. |
| **Pecorino di Pienza** | Italian delis, specialty | From the town of Pienza. Often rubbed with tomato or ash. Excellent. |
| **Pecorino Sardo** | Cheese counters | The Sardinian sheep cheese. Between Toscano and Romano in intensity. |
| **Pecorino Romano** | Most grocery | The hard salty one — a completely different cheese. See its own page. |
| **Manchego** | Most grocery | The Spanish sheep cheese, similar approachability. |

**Label check:** the DOP mark and the grade. *Stagionato* means aged; *fresco*
means young. If it doesn't say, assume young.

**Skip:** buying Pecorino Toscano expecting Pecorino Romano, or the reverse.
They're used for entirely different things.

## Quick FAQ

**Is Pecorino Toscano the same as Pecorino Romano?**
No. Same milk, completely different cheeses. Romano is hard, dry and very
salty. Toscano is mild, buttery and much softer.

**Which grade should I buy?**
*Stagionato* — aged four months or more — for a board.

**Can you eat the rind?**
No. Trim it, especially on wheels rubbed with tomato or ash.

**Is it a good beginner sheep cheese?**
Yes, probably the best. It's approachable in a way Romano isn't.

**How much per person?**
1–2 oz.
