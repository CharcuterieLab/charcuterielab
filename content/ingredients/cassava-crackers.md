## The short version

Crackers and chips made from cassava, the tropical root that also gives us
tapioca. They're grain-free, nut-free, dairy-free and almost flavorless, which
makes cassava the single most useful thing to have on a board when you don't
know your guests' restrictions.

## What it is

Cassava is a starchy root grown across South America, Africa and Southeast
Asia. Ground into flour, it makes a dough that bakes or fries into something
light and neutral. Most of what's sold in the US is a cassava *chip* or
tortilla-style round rather than a flat cracker — dedicated cassava crackers
exist but the shelf is thin and rotates.

**Taste:** very mild, faintly sweet, clean
**Texture:** light and crisp, sturdier than it looks
**Look:** pale, thin, slightly translucent at the edges

Absence is the mechanism. Cassava avoids all eight major allergen groups at
once — no wheat, no nuts, no dairy, no soy, no egg. On a board that has to work
for a mixed group, that's worth more than flavor, and cassava is the only base
in this section that manages it.

## What it pairs with — and why

| Pair it with | Why it works |
|---|---|
| **Guacamole and avocado** | Neutral and sturdy against creamy and rich. The pairing cassava chips were built for. |
| **Fresh goat cheese** | The cracker contributes nothing, so the cheese reads clearly. |
| **Hummus and bean dips** | One of the few gluten-free bases strong enough to scoop with. |
| **Smoked salmon** | Plain enough not to interfere with an expensive topping. |
| **Salsa and pico de gallo** | The tortilla-style rounds are built for this and hold up to the liquid. |
| **Sharp cheddar** | Neutral base, loud cheese. Works exactly as a water cracker would. |
| **Prosciutto and cured meats** | No competing flavor, and it won't snap under a folded slice. |

**Skip it with:** nothing in particular. Cassava is the most agreeable base
here. Its only real weakness is that it's boring on its own, so it needs a
topping with something to say.

## How to prep it for the board

1. **Make it the designated safe base.** Put it in its own dish, away from the
   wheat crackers, and keep a clean knife next to it.
2. **Label it.** "Gluten-free, nut-free" on a small card does more work than
   any styling choice on the board.
3. **Serve 5–6 pieces per person** — more if it's the only option for a guest
   who can't eat the rest.
4. **Cross-contamination matters.** A guest with celiac disease can't use a
   knife that just cut through a baguette. One spare knife solves it.
5. **Put it near the dips**, where its sturdiness is an advantage over almond
   flour crackers.
6. **Fix:** it stales slowly and re-crisps at 300°F for 3 minutes.

**Label check:** "cassava flour" near the top, and check for almond or coconut
if a guest has a nut allergy — several grain-free brands blend them in.

**Skip:** anything labeled grain-free that turns out to be mostly almond flour.
That defeats the reason you bought cassava.

## Quick FAQ

**Is cassava gluten-free?**
Yes. It's a root, not a grain, and contains no gluten at all.

**Is cassava the same as tapioca?**
Tapioca is the starch extracted from cassava. Cassava flour is the whole
ground root, so it behaves differently in baking.

**Are cassava crackers nut-free?**
The plain ones are, but several grain-free brands blend in almond or coconut
flour. Read the box.

**How many per person?**
5–6, and put out extra if it's a guest's only option.

**Where do I find actual cassava crackers, not chips?**
Natural grocers and online. The chip and tortilla formats are far more common
in US stores, and they do the same job on a board.
