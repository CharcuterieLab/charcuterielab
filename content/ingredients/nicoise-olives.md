## The short version

Small, wrinkled, purple-brown olives from Provence, cured in brine with herbs.
They're intensely flavoured for their size, mostly pit by volume, and expensive
per usable ounce. They're also the correct olive for a French board and the best
one with goat cheese.

## What it is

The Cailletier variety grown around Nice, harvested ripe and brine-cured, often
with herbes de Provence. Small — about the size of a large pea — with a
proportionally large pit. Related in style to the Ligurian Taggiasca, which is
frequently sold as a substitute.

**Taste:** rich, nutty, herbal, moderately salty, faintly sour
**Texture:** soft-firm, slightly wrinkled skin
**Look:** small dark purple-brown ovals, often with herbs clinging

The mechanism is concentration in a small package. A Niçoise carries as much
flavour as an olive three times its size, so a small bowl reads as generous. The
herbal note from the cure is what separates it from a Kalamata — it brings thyme
and rosemary to the board without you adding any.

## What it pairs with — and why

| Pair it with | Why it works |
|---|---|
| **Fresh goat cheese and Bucheron** | The Provençal pairing. Chalky lactic tang against herbal salt, and the sizes suit each other. |
| **Anchovies** | Both are salt-cured and both come from the same coast. Salade niçoise, deconstructed. |
| **Baguette** | The base. Nothing more is needed. |
| **Comté and alpine cheeses** | Nutty, sweet, dense cheese with small sharp salty olives. |
| **Tapenade** | The traditional tapenade olive. Serving both is a legitimate French move. |
| **Rosé and dry white wine** | Both carry enough acid to match the brine, and neither has the tannin that would clash with the herbs. |

**Skip it with:** very mild fresh cheeses. Niçoise olives are intense for their
size and will dominate.

## How to prep it for the board

1. **Drain and serve in a small bowl** with a pit bowl beside it.
2. **Warn people about the pits.** They're large relative to the olive and easy
   to bite into.
3. **Serve 0.75 oz per person** — less than a larger olive, because they're more
   intense.
4. **Leave the herbs on.** They're part of the product.
5. **Serve at room temperature.**
6. **Use them where they'll be noticed** — beside goat cheese, not lost in a
   mixed bowl.
7. **Fix:** too expensive? Taggiasca olives from Liguria are nearly identical and
   usually cheaper.

## What to buy

| Product | Where | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Niçoise olives in brine** | Cheese shops, French delis, olive bars | The real thing. Look for herbs in the jar. |
| **Taggiasca olives** | Italian delis | Ligurian cousin, very similar, often better value. |
| **Niçoise in olive oil** | Specialty | Richer, less sharp. Excellent with bread. |
| **Picholine olives** | French delis | The other French board olive — green, firm, nutty. A good companion. |

**Label check:** they should look wrinkled and dark, with visible herbs. Anything
smooth and uniformly black is a different olive.

**Skip:** pitted Niçoise. There's almost nothing left.

## Quick FAQ

**What are Niçoise olives?**
Small dark Provençal olives of the Cailletier variety, brine-cured with herbs.
Intensely flavoured and mostly pit by volume.

**Why are they so small?**
It's the variety. The Cailletier tree produces small fruit, which is why they cost
more per usable ounce.

**What can I use instead?**
Taggiasca olives from Liguria are very similar and usually cheaper.

**What cheese goes with Niçoise olives?**
Fresh goat cheese and aged goat cheeses like Bucheron, plus comté and alpine
cheeses.

**How much per person?**
About three-quarters of an ounce. They're intense, so people take fewer.
