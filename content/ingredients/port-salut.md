## The short version

A soft French cheese in a bright orange rind, made originally by Trappist
monks. It looks like a washed-rind cheese and behaves like a mild one — creamy,
savory, faintly tangy, and nowhere near as strong as its color suggests.

## What it is

Port Salut was created at the Abbey of Notre-Dame de Port-du-Salut in Brittany
in the 1800s, and the recipe was later sold commercially. It's a washed-rind
cheese, but the wash is gentle and the aging short, so the rind bacteria never
develop the pungency of a Munster or an Époisses. The modern orange rind is
colored rather than naturally developed.

**Taste:** mild, creamy, savory, gently tangy
**Texture:** semi-soft, smooth, springy, easy to slice
**Look:** pale ivory paste, smooth bright orange rind

Interrupted development is the mechanism. A washed rind starts the same way
every time — brine, bacteria, orange color — but pungency needs weeks. Port
Salut is sold young, so it stops at savory. That makes it the useful cheese for
a board where you want a washed-rind's look and savory character without asking
anything of your guests.

## What it pairs with — and why

| Pair it with | Why it works |
|---|---|
| **Baguette** | Plain bread under a gentle savory cheese. The French default. |
| **Apple and grapes** | Fresh acid and water against a creamy paste. |
| **Cured ham** | Salt and cure with something mild and rich. |
| **Cornichons** | Sharp vinegar giving a mild cheese an edge. |
| **Walnuts** | Tannin and texture against smooth softness. |
| **Fig jam and honey** | Sweetness works, though it isn't necessary here. |
| **Light reds, dry cider, Chenin Blanc** | Nothing heavy required. |

**Skip it with:** blue cheese and pecorino — Port Salut is mild and loses. It
also disappears next to a real washed-rind like Époisses, which makes serving
both on one board a mistake.

## How to prep it for the board

1. **Cut into thin wedges or slices** with the orange rind edge showing. The
   color is most of the visual appeal.
2. **Temper 30 minutes.**
3. **Serve 1–2 oz per person.**
4. **Use it where you want a washed-rind look without the smell** — a board for
   a mixed or cautious crowd.
5. **Trim the rind.** It's edible but rubbery and colored rather than
   flavorful.
6. **Place it in the middle range of the board**, between the mild and the
   strong.
7. **Fix:** if it seems flat, it's cold or it's next to something too strong.
   Move it beside the fruit.

## What to buy

| Product | Where | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Port Salut** | Most grocery stores, cheese case | The widely distributed commercial version. Consistent and cheap. |
| **Saint-Paulin** | Cheese counters, grocery | Essentially the same cheese under the generic name. Often better value. |
| **Trappist-made abbey cheeses** | Cheese counters, specialty | Chimay, Maredsous and similar. More character, same family. |
| **Reblochon** | Cheese counters | A real French washed-rind with genuine flavor — see its own page. |
| **Havarti** | Grocery cheese case | If mildness is what you're after, this does it without the rind. |
| **Fontina Val d'Aosta** | Cheese counters | The step up in earthiness — see its own page. |

**Label check:** there isn't much to check. This is a consistent industrial
cheese, and the main decision is whether you want a mild washed-rind at all.

**Skip:** expecting it to taste like the abbey cheeses it descends from. The
modern commercial version is deliberately mild.

## Quick FAQ

**Does Port Salut smell?**
Barely. It's a washed-rind cheese sold young, so it never develops the
pungency of Munster or Époisses.

**Can you eat the orange rind?**
It's edible but rubbery and artificially colored. Most people trim it.

**Is it a monastery cheese?**
It began as one, at a Trappist abbey in Brittany in the 1800s. The modern
version is made commercially.

**Port Salut or Havarti?**
Similar mildness. Port Salut is more savory and has a more interesting look;
Havarti is butterier.

**How much per person?**
1–2 oz.
