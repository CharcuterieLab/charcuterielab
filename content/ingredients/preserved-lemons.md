## The short version

Lemons quartered, buried in salt and left for a month. The salt breaks down the
pith, so the bitterness vanishes and the rind turns soft and translucent. What is
left tastes more of lemon than a lemon does, without any of the sourness. Chopped
fine over labneh or olives, a teaspoon lifts the whole savoury half of a board.

## What it is

A Moroccan preserve, sold in jars and increasingly in ordinary supermarkets. The
lemons sit in their own juice and salt, sometimes with cinnamon, clove or bay. The
pulp is usually discarded; the peel is the ingredient.

**Taste:** deep lemon oil, salt, a faint funk, and no bitterness at all
**Texture:** soft, yielding peel, nothing like fresh zest
**Look:** translucent yellow, slumped, sitting in cloudy brine

The mechanism is salt breaking down pith. Lemon bitterness lives in the white
albedo, and a month in high-salt brine degrades those compounds while the aromatic
oils in the outer peel survive. The result is the top note of a lemon without the
sharpness or the bitterness — a flavour you cannot get any other way.

## What it pairs with — and why

| Pair it with | Why it works |
|---|---|
| **Labneh** | Chopped fine over cool yogurt with oil. Salt-sour on lactic-sour, and it makes a plain bowl look considered. |
| **Harissa** | Both Maghrebi staples. The lemon's salt-brightness stops the harissa reading as only heat. |
| **Castelvetrano olives** | Buttery green olives tossed with chopped preserved lemon is a two-minute upgrade on the best olive there is. |
| **Feta** | Salt on salt works when the second salt brings citrus with it. |
| **Hummus** | Solves the flatness problem better than more lemon juice does, because it adds oil flavour as well as acid. |
| **Marinated artichokes** | Both oily and both a little bitter. The lemon ties them together. |

**Skip it with:** sweet preserves and dessert items. This is a savoury seasoning.

## How to prep it for the board

1. **Scrape out the pulp** and discard it. The peel is what you want.
2. **Rinse the peel** briefly under cold water to take the edge off the salt.
3. **Slice it very thin or chop it fine.** Whole pieces are too much.
4. **Scatter it over dips and olives** rather than serving it alone.
5. **Serve 0.25 oz per person.**
6. **Save the brine.** A spoonful in a dressing is excellent.
7. **Fix:** still too salty? Rinse again and toss the peel in olive oil for a
   minute before using it.

## What to buy

| Product | Where | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Jarred preserved lemons** | Middle Eastern grocers, larger supermarkets | The default. Look for whole quarters in cloudy brine. |
| **Beldi preserved lemons** | Moroccan and specialty grocers | Small thin-skinned Moroccan lemons. The reference version. |
| **Preserved lemon paste** | Specialty | Already chopped and blended. Convenient, less pretty on a board. |
| **Preserved Meyer lemons** | Specialty, farmers markets | Sweeter and more floral. Excellent and expensive. |
| **Homemade** | Make it | Lemons, coarse salt, a clean jar and a month. Almost free. |

**Label check:** lemons, salt, water. Anything with citric acid or preservative has
been shortcut.

**Skip:** jars where the peel has gone brown and the brine is clear. That is old
stock.

## Quick FAQ

**Do you eat the whole preserved lemon?**
You eat the rind. Most cooks scrape out and discard the salty pulp, then rinse and
slice the peel.

**Are preserved lemons very salty?**
Yes, straight from the jar. Rinsing them briefly under cold water fixes it without
losing the flavour.

**What do they taste like?**
Intensely lemony but not sour, with the bitterness gone and a soft, almost floral
saltiness in its place.

**Can I make them at home?**
Yes. Quarter lemons, pack them with a lot of coarse salt and their own juice, and
leave them a month at room temperature.

**How much per person?**
A quarter ounce, chopped fine. A little goes a long way.
