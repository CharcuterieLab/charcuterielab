## The short version

Crackers made jet black with activated charcoal. They're a British cheese-shop
staple, they're expensive, and they taste much plainer than they look — which
is exactly right. On a board of cream, tan and dark red, one black cracker does
more visual work than any garnish.

## What it is

Activated charcoal is a fine, flavorless powder. Added to a standard cracker
dough it turns the whole thing matte black without meaningfully changing the
taste. The British cheese trade adopted them because a very dark cracker makes
a pale cheese look better — the same reason slate boards became fashionable.

**Taste:** mild, plain, faintly savory
**Texture:** crisp and snappy, like a good water cracker
**Look:** matte jet black, the highest-contrast item available for a board

Contrast is the mechanism, and it's honest about being a visual one. Almost
everything on a charcuterie board lives between cream and russet. A black
cracker under a white goat cheese is the single strongest visual pairing you
can make, and it's why these photograph so well.

## What it pairs with — and why

| Pair it with | Why it works |
|---|---|
| **Fresh white goat cheese** | Maximum contrast: chalk white on matte black. The bite is also good, but the look is the point. |
| **Burrata and fresh mozzarella** | Same logic, softer shapes. Spectacular on camera. |
| **Blue cheese** | The blue veining reads much more clearly against black than against a pale cracker. |
| **Brie and Camembert** | Mild cheese, mild cracker, dramatic picture. |
| **Smoked salmon** | Pink against black. A very old visual pairing in fine dining. |
| **Honey and figs** | Adds a third color. Three-tone bites are what makes a board look styled. |
| **Champagne and sparkling wine** | This is a special-occasion cracker; serve it like one. |

**Skip it with:** nothing on flavor grounds — they're too mild to clash. The
real caution is quantity. A handful is striking; a whole section of black
crackers looks like a Halloween board.

## How to prep it for the board

1. **Use a few, not a lot.** Six to eight on a large board is plenty. This is
   an accent, not a base.
2. **Place them where a pale cheese sits.** Directly under or beside the
   whitest thing on the board.
3. **Serve 2–3 per person** and put a normal cracker out for volume.
4. **Don't cluster them.** Two here, three there — scattered contrast reads as
   composition; a black pile reads as a mistake.
5. **Warn nobody and explain to everybody.** People will ask. "Activated
   charcoal, it's a British cheese-shop thing" is the whole answer.
6. **Fix:** they behave like any thin cracker. Re-crisp at 300°F for 3 minutes.

## What to buy

| Product | Where | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **The Fine Cheese Co. Charcoal Squares** (4.9 oz) | Formaggio Kitchen, US cheese shops, online | The most reliably available in the US. Excellent quality. |
| **Miller's Damsels Charcoal Wafers** | UK cheese shops, British import sites | The other well-known maker. Harder to get stateside. |
| **Cheese-counter charcoal crackers** | Whole Foods, Murray's, local cheese shops | Ask at the counter — these live there, not in the cracker aisle. |
| **Squid ink crackers** | Italian and Spanish specialty shops | A different route to the same black. These *do* taste of something. |
| **Activated charcoal powder** | Health food stores, Amazon | The DIY route. Half a teaspoon in a standard cracker dough. |

**Label check:** activated charcoal or vegetable carbon in the ingredients. If
the color comes from squid ink or cocoa, it will taste of that.

**Skip:** charcoal-colored snack crackers with flavoring added. The whole point
is a plain cracker that happens to be black.

## Quick FAQ

**Is activated charcoal safe to eat?**
In food quantities, yes — it's used as a food coloring. It can bind to some
medications, so anyone on prescription medication may want to skip them.

**Do charcoal crackers taste like charcoal?**
No. Activated charcoal is flavorless. They taste like a plain cracker.

**Why are they black?**
Purely visual. British cheesemongers use them because they make pale cheese
look better.

**How many should I put out?**
Six to eight on a large board, scattered rather than piled. They're an accent.

**Where do I buy them in the US?**
Cheese counters and specialty shops rather than supermarket cracker aisles. The
Fine Cheese Co. version is the most findable.
