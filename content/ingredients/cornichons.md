## The short version

The small sour pickles that come with every French charcuterie plate, and they're
there for a reason. Pâté and rillettes are almost pure fat; a cornichon's sharp
vinegar cuts it so completely that you can eat a second slice. On a meat board
they're not a garnish, they're a function.

## What it is

Tiny gherkin cucumbers picked young and pickled in white wine vinegar with
tarragon, pearl onions, mustard seed and peppercorns. Distinctly French — the
Polish and German equivalents are usually sweeter or dill-forward. Sharp, never
sweet.

**Taste:** sharply sour, tarragon-herbal, mildly salty
**Texture:** very crunchy, firm all the way through
**Look:** small bumpy green pickles, one to two inches, in cloudy brine

The mechanism is acid at high concentration in a small bite. Fat coats the palate
and dulls it; acetic acid strips it. A cornichon delivers enough vinegar in one
crunch to reset the mouth completely, and its size means you can do that between
every bite without filling up.

## What it pairs with — and why

| Pair it with | Why it works |
|---|---|
| **Pâté de campagne and terrines** | The classic. Rich liver fat needs acid to be edible in quantity, and this is the sharpest small thing available. |
| **Rillettes** | Same logic, more so. Rillettes are pure rendered fat and need cutting. |
| **Saucisson sec and dry salami** | Fat and salt sliced by vinegar and crunch. |
| **Dijon mustard** | Two acids at different intensities, served side by side on every French plate. |
| **Comté and gruyère** | Nutty, dense, fatty cheese with a sour crunch. The Alpine ploughman's. |
| **Raclette and melted cheese** | The traditional accompaniment, and the reason both are on the same menu. |

**Skip it with:** delicate fresh cheeses and sweet jams. Cornichons are aggressive
and small enough to be everywhere on a board.

## How to prep it for the board

1. **Drain them** and serve in a small bowl or scattered beside the pâté.
2. **Don't rinse them.** The brine is the point.
3. **Serve 0.5 oz per person** — three or four.
4. **Put them next to the pâté and the mustard**, which is where they earn their
   place.
5. **Buy the sour kind, not the sweet.** Read the label; some brands add sugar.
6. **Serve cold or at room temperature** — both work.
7. **Fix:** soft and limp? That's an old jar. They should snap.

## What to buy

| Product | Where | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Maille cornichons** | Any grocery | The widely available standard. Properly sour and crunchy. |
| **French cornichons extra-fins** | French delis, specialty | Smaller and crunchier. The best grade. |
| **Trader Joe's cornichons** | Trader Joe's | Cheap and good. |
| **Cornichons with pearl onions** | Grocery, specialty | Two pickles in one jar. Good value on a meat board. |
| **Polish or German gherkins** | European delis | Often sweeter or dill-forward. A different and less useful thing here. |

**Label check:** vinegar and tarragon, no sugar. "Sweet gherkins" are a different
product entirely.

**Skip:** anything soft in the jar, and any brand that lists sugar high in the
ingredients.

## Quick FAQ

**What are cornichons?**
Tiny French pickled gherkins, sharply sour and crunchy, usually flavoured with
tarragon and mustard seed.

**Are cornichons the same as gherkins?**
They're the same cucumber, but cornichons are picked smaller and pickled sour in
the French style. Many gherkins are sweetened.

**What do you eat cornichons with?**
Pâté, terrines, rillettes and dry salami — anything rich and fatty that needs acid
to cut it.

**Why does a charcuterie board need pickles?**
Acid. A board of cured meat and cheese is fat and salt, and pickles are the
cheapest way to cut through both.

**How many per person?**
Three or four — about half an ounce.
