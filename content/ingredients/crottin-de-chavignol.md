## The short version

A small round goat cheese from the village of Chavignol in the Loire, about the
size of a large walnut. Young it's soft and lemony; aged it turns hard, dark and
intensely nutty. Both are good, and grilled on toast it's a French bistro
classic.

## What it is

Crottin de Chavignol AOP is made from raw goat's milk in Sancerre country. The
name is unflattering — *crottin* means dung, describing the small dark shape of
a well-aged one. It's sold at every stage from ten days to several months, and
the cheese at each stage is genuinely different.

**Young:** white, soft, lemony, milky
**Medium (*demi-sec*):** firmer, more concentrated, faint blue-grey rind
**Aged (*sec* / *repassé*):** hard, dark brown, intensely nutty and savory
**Look:** a small flat-topped cylinder, white to dark brown depending on age

Moisture loss over a small volume is the mechanism, and it's why this cheese
transforms so completely. A large wheel takes months to change noticeably. A
crottin is small enough that a few weeks halves its weight, concentrating
everything — which means one cheese offers three distinct experiences depending
on when you buy it.

## What it pairs with — and why

| Pair it with | Why it works |
|---|---|
| **Sancerre** | The same village makes both. Textbook regional pairing and one of the great ones. |
| **Crostini, grilled warm** | *Chèvre chaud* — halved crottin grilled on toast until bubbling, over salad. Standard bistro fare and easy to do on a board. |
| **Honey** | Sweet against goat acid, especially with the aged version. |
| **Walnuts** | Tannin and nut against a nutty, dense little cheese. |
| **Fresh thyme** | Goat cheese takes herbs better than any other family. |
| **Green salad with vinaigrette** | Acid on acid, which works here because both are sharp and clean. |
| **Cherry preserve or fig jam** | Fruit sweetness with a tangy paste. |

**Skip it with:** blue cheese and anything garlicky. Crottin is small and
precise and it gets lost easily.

## How to prep it for the board

1. **Ask the counter what age you're getting.** Young, demi-sec and sec are
   effectively three different cheeses, and the label often doesn't say.
2. **Serve them whole or halved.** They're small — a whole crottin on a board
   looks deliberate.
3. **Temper 30 minutes.**
4. **Serve about 1 oz per person** — roughly one crottin per two people.
5. **Grill some of them.** Halve, put on crostini, broil three minutes until
   bubbling and golden. It's the best thing you can do with this cheese and it
   takes five minutes.
6. **Buy two ages if you can** and put them side by side. The comparison is
   striking.
7. **Fix:** hard, dark and dry isn't a fault. That's *sec*, and it's a
   delicacy. Grate it or grill it.

**Label check:** the AOP mark and, ideally, an age or a description. A pale
white crottin is young; a grey-blue one is demi-sec; a hard brown one is sec.

**Skip:** assuming a hard dark crottin has gone bad. That's the aged version and
it's the one connoisseurs buy.

## Quick FAQ

**What does *crottin* mean?**
Dung — a description of the small dark shape of an aged one. French cheese
naming is not always flattering.

**Which age should I buy?**
Demi-sec for a board, young for grilling, sec if you want intensity.

**What is *chèvre chaud*?**
Grilled goat cheese on toast, usually served over dressed salad. Halve a
crottin, put it on crostini, broil three minutes.

**Can you eat the rind?**
Yes, at every age.

**How much per person?**
About 1 oz — roughly one crottin per two people.
