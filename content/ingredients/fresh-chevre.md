## The short version

Young, unaged goat's milk cheese, usually sold as a log. Bright white, tangy,
slightly chalky. It's the cheapest way to put real acidity on a board — and
acidity is the thing most boards are short of.

## What it is

*Chèvre* just means goat in French, but in a US cheese case it means the fresh,
unaged version: goat's milk set with a culture and a little rennet, drained,
and shaped. No rind, no aging, sold within days. Goat's milk has smaller fat
globules and a different fatty acid profile than cow's milk, which is where the
distinctive tang comes from.

**Taste:** bright, tangy, lemony, faintly grassy
**Texture:** soft and chalky, crumbles then spreads
**Look:** stark white log or puck, sometimes rolled in herbs or ash

Acid is the mechanism. Goat's milk cheese carries more perceptible acidity than
almost anything else on a board, and acid does two jobs at once — it cuts fat
and it makes sweetness read as brighter. That's why honey on chèvre tastes more
like honey than honey on anything else.

## What it pairs with — and why

| Pair it with | Why it works |
|---|---|
| **Honey** | The pairing. Acid sharpens sweetness; sweetness rounds acid. Nothing else is this efficient. |
| **Fig jam** | Same mechanism, more body. Dense fruit paste on tangy chalk. |
| **Seeded crackers** | Bitter seed oil against bright acid, with crunch against chalk. |
| **Fruit and nut crisps** | The cracker brings the sweetness and the nut; the cheese brings the tang. Two items, finished bite. |
| **Beets and walnuts** | The salad, taken apart. Earthy and tannic against sharp. |
| **Fresh herbs — thyme, chives** | Chèvre takes herbs better than any other fresh cheese. |
| **Sancerre, Sauvignon Blanc** | The textbook regional pairing. High acid meets high acid. |

**Skip it with:** blue cheese, which buries it, and everything crackers, where
garlic fights the tang. Chèvre is bright rather than loud, and loud things beat
it.

## How to prep it for the board

1. **Temper 20 minutes.** Cold chèvre is chalky and mute; warm, the tang and
   the grassiness come forward.
2. **Slice the log with dental floss or a thin wire**, not a knife — a blade
   drags and smears. Floss gives clean discs.
3. **Or crumble it deliberately** into a loose pile. Both look intentional; a
   half-smeared log doesn't.
4. **Serve 1–2 oz per person.** A 4 oz log serves three to four.
5. **Drizzle honey over it before serving** and add cracked pepper. Thirty
   seconds, and it stops being a plain white log.
6. **Roll it in chopped herbs, pistachios or cracked pepper** for a bigger
   upgrade — press the log into the mixture on a plate.
7. **Fix:** dry and crumbly at the ends? Trim them. The interior will be fine.

**Label check:** buy it firm and moist, not crumbling at the edges. A dry,
cracked log has been sitting.

**Skip:** the sweetened dessert-flavored logs — cranberry-cinnamon and similar
— on a savory board. There's a page for cranberry goat cheese; it's a different
job.

## Quick FAQ

**What's the difference between chèvre and goat cheese?**
Chèvre is French for goat. In practice, a US cheese case uses it to mean the
fresh, unaged log — as opposed to aged goat cheeses like Bucheron or Humboldt
Fog.

**Why does goat cheese taste tangy?**
Goat's milk has a different fatty acid profile than cow's milk, and the fresh
cheese is high in lactic acid.

**How do I slice it without smearing?**
Dental floss or thin wire. A knife drags.

**How much per person?**
1–2 oz. A 4 oz log serves three to four.

**What's the single best pairing?**
Honey, with cracked pepper. It takes thirty seconds and it's the most reliable
bite on a board.
