## The short version

Fresh grape juice simmered for hours until it turns into a dark, glossy
syrup. Saba tastes like grapes, raisins and a touch of tartness, and it's the
sweet ancestor of balsamic vinegar. Drizzle it over Parmigiano-Reggiano or
ricotta and it does what balsamic glaze does, only rounder and more grapey.

## What it is

Saba (also sapa or mosto cotto) has been made in Italy since Roman times. The
must from freshly crushed grapes, often Trebbiano or Lambrusco, is slowly
cooked in open pots until it reduces to about a third or less of its volume.
It isn't fermented or aged into vinegar, so it stays sweet. In Emilia-Romagna
it was a traditional sweetener before sugar was common.

**Taste:** concentrated grape and raisin, with caramel and a soft tartness
**Texture:** thick and syrupy, like warm honey
**Look:** deep mahogany, glossy

Reduction is the mechanism. Cooking off water concentrates the grape's
natural sugars and acids together, and long, gentle heat browns the sugar
for caramel notes. Because the acid stays in proportion, saba has a gentle
tartness that keeps it from tasting like plain syrup, which is what lets it
cut through a salty, fatty cheese.

## What it pairs with — and why

| Pair it with | Why it works |
|---|---|
| **Parmigiano-Reggiano** | Sweet and tart against salty crystals. The Emilian pairing. |
| **Ricotta or burrata** | Dark syrup on white cream, sweet against mild. |
| **Pecorino Toscano** | Salty sheep's milk balanced by fruit sugar. |
| **Fresh figs** | Grape and fig, a fall classic. |
| **Prosciutto** | Sweet-salty contrast, like a grape-syrup take on melon and ham. |
| **Lambrusco or a Sangiovese** | Grape on grape, from the same part of Italy. |

**Skip it with:** balsamic glaze on the same board. They do the same job,
and two dark drizzles look muddy.

## How to prep it for the board

1. **Drizzle it just before serving.** It slowly soaks into soft cheese.
2. **Use about a teaspoon per person.** It's sweet and concentrated.
3. **Drizzle in thin lines** over the cheese, or pool a little on a small
   plate with a spoon.
4. **Put it on the hard cheese and the ricotta,** not on crackers.
5. **Keep the bottle on the side** so guests can add more.
6. **Fix:** if it has thickened in the cold, warm the bottle in hot water for
   a few minutes.

## Quick FAQ

**What is saba?**
Grape must, the fresh juice of crushed grapes, slowly cooked down to a thick
syrup. It's an old Italian condiment, also called sapa or mosto cotto.

**Is saba the same as balsamic vinegar?**
No, but it's the starting point. Traditional balsamic is cooked must that has
been fermented and aged for years. Saba is the sweet syrup without the
vinegar stage.

**What does saba taste like?**
Like a dark, concentrated grape and raisin syrup with a gentle tartness.
Sweeter and softer than balsamic glaze.

**Does saba contain alcohol?**
No. It's cooked from fresh juice before fermentation.

**How long does it keep?**
Months in the pantry once opened. It's mostly sugar.
