## The short version

A soft, grainy fresh cheese made from the whey that's left after making other
cheese — *ricotta* means "recooked." Mild, faintly sweet, and transformed by
five minutes with a whisk. Whipped ricotta on crostini with honey is the
cheapest impressive thing on any board.

## What it is

When curd is separated for mozzarella or provolone, protein stays behind in the
whey. Reheating that whey makes those proteins coagulate into fine, soft curds
— ricotta. Because it's made from a by-product it's cheap, and because it's
never aged it's mild.

**Taste:** milky, faintly sweet, clean, lightly salted
**Texture:** soft and grainy, loose, spoonable
**Look:** white, slightly lumpy, moist

Particle size is the mechanism, and it's why whipping matters so much here.
Ricotta's curds are fine but distinct, which reads as grainy on the tongue.
Five minutes in a food processor breaks them down into something silky, and the
same cheese goes from a baking ingredient to a board centerpiece.

## What it pairs with — and why

| Pair it with | Why it works |
|---|---|
| **Honey and cracked pepper** | Sweet, sharp and mild. The classic Italian *ricotta e miele*, and it takes thirty seconds. |
| **Crostini** | It's a spread, so it needs a rigid base that won't go soggy under it. |
| **Lemon zest and olive oil** | Acid and fat lift a cheese that has neither in quantity. |
| **Figs and peaches** | Ripe fruit against clean milk. A summer board default. |
| **Pistachios** | Crunch and a little bitterness against something soft and sweet. |
| **Prosciutto** | Salt and cure over mild cream — much better than it sounds. |
| **Prosecco, Vermentino, moscato** | Light and slightly sweet wines. |

**Skip it with:** blue cheese and strong cured meats, which flatten it, and
crackers that snap under a spoonful. Ricotta needs a base with structure.

## How to prep it for the board

1. **Whip it.** Five minutes in a food processor with a splash of olive oil and
   a pinch of salt. This is the single highest-value step on this page.
2. **Drain it first if it's watery** — 20 minutes in a fine sieve.
3. **Spread it in a shallow bowl**, swirl the top with the back of a spoon, and
   pool olive oil in the swirl.
4. **Season it.** Salt, pepper, lemon zest. Plain ricotta on a board tastes
   unfinished.
5. **Serve 1–2 oz per person.** A 15 oz tub covers eight to ten.
6. **Put crostini right beside it** — it can't be eaten without a base, unlike
   a firm cheese.
7. **Fix:** grainy and dull? Whip it. Watery? Drain it. Bland? Salt and oil.
   Almost every ricotta problem has one of those three answers.

| **Ricotta salata** | Cheese counters | The pressed, salted, aged version — firm and grateable. A different ingredient. |
| **Trader Joe's Whole Milk Ricotta** | Trader Joe's | Cheap and fine, especially once whipped. |

**Label check:** whole milk, and as short an ingredient list as possible. Gums
and stabilizers are common in the cheap tubs and they make whipping less
effective.

**Skip:** part-skim ricotta for a board. The fat is what makes it taste of
anything.

## Quick FAQ

**Should I whip ricotta for a cheese board?**
Yes. Five minutes in a food processor with oil and salt turns a grainy tub into
a silky spread. It's the difference between ignored and finished first.

**What is ricotta actually made from?**
Whey left over from making other cheeses, reheated so the remaining proteins
coagulate.

**Is ricotta the same as cottage cheese?**
No. Cottage cheese is made from milk curds and has larger, wetter lumps.
Ricotta is finer and made from whey.

**How much per person?**
1–2 oz. A 15 oz tub serves eight to ten.

**What's ricotta salata?**
Ricotta that's been pressed, salted and aged into a firm, grateable cheese. A
different ingredient with a different use.
