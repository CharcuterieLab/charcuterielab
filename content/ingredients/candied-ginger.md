## The short version

Young ginger simmered in sugar syrup until translucent, then dried and usually
tossed in sugar. It reads as a sweet at first and then a spice, and the warmth
lingers after the sugar has gone. That delayed heat is why it works on a rich
board: one piece resets your mouth between the fifth and sixth thing you eat.

## What it is

A preserve, not a confection. The syrup draws water out of the ginger and replaces
it with sugar, which is what makes the pieces translucent and shelf-stable. Young
ginger gives tender pieces; older ginger gives fibrous ones.

**Taste:** sweet first, then a clean building warmth
**Texture:** chewy and slightly fibrous, with a sugar crust
**Look:** translucent pale gold, sugar-frosted

The mechanism is capsaicin's cousin. Ginger's heat comes from gingerol, which is
oil-soluble and slow to register — unlike chili, it builds after the sweetness has
faded rather than arriving with it. That staggered timing is exactly what a rich
board needs, because it gives the palate a second act instead of another sugar hit.

## What it pairs with — and why

| Pair it with | Why it works |
|---|---|
| **Dark chocolate** | Bitter and warm. The pairing that sells itself, and it is the reason chocolate-covered ginger exists. |
| **Aged gouda** | Butterscotch sweetness and crystal crunch against sugar and heat. The best cheese pairing here by some distance. |
| **Stilton and blue cheese** | Salt and funk need sweetness; ginger brings sweetness plus a warm finish that fig jam cannot. |
| **Gingersnaps** | Doubling down. Two forms of the same spice reads as a theme rather than a repeat. |
| **Pears** | Cool, wet and floral against warm and chewy. A classic autumn combination. |
| **Mascarpone** | Cream flattens the heat just enough. Chop the ginger fine and stir it through. |

**Skip it with:** delicate fresh cheeses and fine dry-cured ham. Ginger walks over
both.

## How to prep it for the board

1. **Slice large pieces thin.** A whole chunk is a lot of chew and a lot of heat.
2. **Serve it in a small dish** or scattered with the sweet items.
3. **Keep it away from anything moist** — sugar-coated ginger goes sticky fast.
4. **Chop some finely** to scatter over mascarpone or chocolate.
5. **Serve 0.25 oz per person** — two or three pieces.
6. **Pair it with something cold** if the room is warm.
7. **Fix:** unpleasantly fibrous? That is old ginger. Slice across the grain as
   thinly as you can and it becomes edible again.

## What to buy

| Product | Where | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Crystallised ginger chunks** | Any grocery, baking aisle | The default. Cheap, keeps forever, easy to handle. |
| **Australian glace ginger** | Specialty grocers | Larger, softer, more expensive and much better. |
| **Uncrystallised ginger** | Health food shops | No sugar coating. More intense and less sticky-sweet. |
| **Stem ginger in syrup** | British import shelves | Soft, wet, excellent. Needs a bowl and a spoon. |
| **Chocolate-covered ginger** | Confectioners | Does the dark chocolate pairing for you. Good on a dessert board. |

**Label check:** ginger and sugar. Nothing else needs to be there.

**Skip:** small hard nuggets that rattle in the bag. They are old and fibrous.

## Quick FAQ

**Is candied ginger spicy?**
It has real warmth, yes — the sugar softens it but does not remove it. The heat
builds after the sweetness fades.

**What cheese goes with candied ginger?**
Aged gouda and blue cheese. Both carry enough sweetness of their own to meet it,
and enough salt to balance it.

**Crystallised or uncrystallised?**
Crystallised is coated in sugar and drier. Uncrystallised is softer and more
intense. For a board, crystallised is easier to pick up.

**Is it the same as stem ginger in syrup?**
Same idea, wetter. Stem ginger is excellent but needs a bowl and a spoon.

**How much per person?**
A quarter ounce — two or three pieces.
