## The short version

A Levantine blend of dried wild thyme, toasted sesame seeds, sumac and salt. It's
herbal, nutty and distinctly sour, and a pinch over feta, hummus or a dish of
olive oil is the fastest way to make a board look like somebody thought about it.
Two dollars a bag.

## What it is

Dried thyme or oregano (traditionally wild Levantine hyssop), toasted sesame
seeds, ground sumac, and salt. Regional versions vary: **Lebanese** za'atar is
sumac-heavy and reddish; **Palestinian** is thyme-forward and greener;
**Jordanian** often adds more sesame.

**Taste:** herbal, sour, nutty, savoury
**Texture:** coarse and dry, with visible sesame
**Look:** dusty green-red, flecked with pale sesame

The mechanism is sumac's acidity in dry form. Almost every way of adding acid to a
board involves liquid — vinegar, citrus, pickle brine. Sumac is a dried sour berry,
so za'atar delivers real tartness with no moisture, which means it can be sprinkled
directly onto bread, cheese or oil without softening anything.

## What it pairs with — and why

| Pair it with | Why it works |
|---|---|
| **Whipped feta** | Salt and cream with a dry sour herb dusted over. The standard restaurant finish. |
| **Hummus** | Za'atar and olive oil is the traditional topping, and it adds the acid hummus lacks. |
| **Olive oil** | A dish of oil with za'atar stirred in, and bread to dip. Two ingredients, one course. |
| **Fresh goat cheese** | Roll a log in it, or scatter over. Tang against tang, with sesame for texture. |
| **Pita chips and flatbread** | *Manakish* — flatbread baked with za'atar and oil — is where it comes from. |
| **Cucumber and raw vegetables** | The Levantine breakfast plate, and it works cold on a board. |

**Skip it with:** delicate aged cheeses and sweet elements. Za'atar is herbal and
sour and belongs on the savoury side.

## How to prep it for the board

1. **Stir it into a shallow dish of olive oil**, with bread beside it. That's the
   traditional service and the best one.
2. **Or scatter it dry** over feta, hummus, or a goat cheese log.
3. **Serve a small dish of it plain** so guests can dip.
4. **Use a pinch per dish**, not a spoonful — the sumac is sour.
5. **Taste it first.** Blends vary enormously in salt and sumac.
6. **Buy from a Middle Eastern grocer** if you can. It's cheaper and much fresher.
7. **Fix:** dusty and flat? Old blend. Za'atar fades within a year, and thyme is
   the first thing to go.

| **Burlap & Barrel za'atar** | Specialty, online | Single-origin blend. Excellent and pricier. |
| **Supermarket za'atar** | Grocery, spice aisle | Usually oregano-heavy and salt-heavy. Fine, not exciting. |

**Label check:** sesame should be visible in the bag, and sumac should be listed.
A blend that's mostly salt and oregano is a cheap one.

**Skip:** anything dusty and uniformly brown. You should see distinct green, red
and pale flecks.

## Quick FAQ

**What is za'atar?**
A Levantine blend of dried thyme, toasted sesame seeds, sumac and salt. Herbal,
nutty and sour.

**Is za'atar spicy?**
No. It's herbal and tart, not hot.

**How do I use za'atar on a board?**
Stir it into a dish of olive oil for dipping, or scatter it over feta, hummus or a
goat cheese log.

**What is sumac?**
A dried, ground sour berry that provides za'atar's tartness. It's how the blend
delivers acid without any liquid.

**Does za'atar contain allergens?**
Sesame, which is a declared allergen in most countries. Worth mentioning when you
put it out.
