## The short version

Good tinned sardines are a genuine delicacy in Spain and Portugal, where the
best tins are dated like wine and aged for years. Serve them in the open tin
with saltines, lemon and hot sauce — it's the cheapest luxury board you can put
out.

## What it is

Sardines are small oily fish, cooked and packed in olive oil, water or sauce.
The Iberian *conservas* tradition treats them seriously: fish are hand-packed,
and premium tins are laid down to age, during which the oil and flesh mellow
and the texture improves. Portuguese, Spanish and Galician tins are the
benchmark.

**Taste:** rich, oily, savoury, briny, mineral
**Texture:** soft and yielding, flaking cleanly, sometimes with edible bones
**Look:** silver-blue skin, pale flesh, glossy with oil

Oil packing is the mechanism, and it's why tinned sardines improve where most
tinned food degrades. The oil excludes air and slowly permeates the flesh, so
proteins soften and flavours marry over months or years. A five-year-old tin
from a good producer is a better product than a fresh one.

## What it pairs with — and why

| Pair it with | Why it works |
|---|---|
| **Saltines** | The correct base. Cheap, salty, dry, and it doesn't compete. Genuinely the classic. |
| **Lemon** | Squeeze over at the table. Acid is what makes oily fish sing. |
| **Hot sauce or chili crisp** | Heat against oil. A modern standard and very good. |
| **Pickled onion and cornichons** | Acid and crunch against soft oily fish. |
| **Butter on dark bread** | The Iberian and French service — butter first, then fish. |
| **Flaky salt and cracked pepper** | Finishing seasoning — good tinned fish needs almost nothing else. |
| **Vinho verde, Albariño, dry cider, cold lager** | Crisp, acidic and cold. |

**Skip it with:** delicate fresh cheeses and blue cheese. Tinned fish and cheese
is a hard pairing generally — build a tinned fish corner rather than mixing.

## How to prep it for the board

1. **Serve them in the open tin.** This is the point. Set the tin on the board
   with the lid rolled back and a small fork beside it.
2. **Put saltines and lemon right next to it.**
3. **Don't drain the oil.** It's part of the product and people will want it on
   their cracker.
4. **Serve two to three fillets per person.**
5. **Open the tin at the table if you can** — the moment is half the appeal.
6. **Build a tinned fish corner** rather than scattering: two or three tins,
   crackers, lemon, pickles, butter.
7. **Fix:** if the tin is unappealing on a wooden board, set it on a small plate
   with the oil contained.

**Label check:** packed in olive oil rather than soybean oil, and a country of
origin from Portugal, Spain, France or Norway. Some premium tins carry a vintage
date.

**Skip:** sardines in tomato or mustard sauce for a board unless that's
deliberate. Plain olive oil is the versatile one.

## Quick FAQ

**Do you eat the bones?**
Yes — they're soft, edible and full of calcium. Skinless-boneless tins exist if
you'd rather not.

**Why do people age tinned sardines?**
The oil slowly permeates the flesh, softening texture and marrying flavours.
Premium Portuguese tins are dated and laid down like wine.

**What crackers go with them?**
Saltines. It's the traditional and still the best answer — cheap, dry and
neutral.

**How much per person?**
Two to three fillets, or roughly a tin per three people.

**Olive oil or water packed?**
Olive oil, for a board. Water-packed is a cooking product.
