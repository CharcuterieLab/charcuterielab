## The short version

A hard, dry, very salty Mexican cheese that crumbles into shards. It's what
goes on elote and esquites, and on a board it does the job pecorino does —
seasoning whatever it touches rather than being eaten alone.

## What it is

Cotija is named for a town in Michoacán. Made from cow's milk, pressed, dry-salted
and aged from a few months to a year. Young cotija (*fresco*) is moist and
crumbly; aged cotija (*añejo*) is hard, dry and grateable.

**Taste:** very salty, sharp, milky, faintly tangy
**Texture:** dry and firm, crumbles into irregular shards
**Look:** stark white, matte, no rind

Salt concentration is the mechanism. Cotija is dry-salted rather than brined,
and aging drives out moisture while the salt stays put — so the salt-to-cheese
ratio climbs. That's why a small amount seasons a whole bite, and why it needs
something sweet, fatty or acidic beside it rather than another salty thing.

## What it pairs with — and why

| Pair it with | Why it works |
|---|---|
| **Grilled corn, lime and chili** | Elote taken apart. Sweet, sour, hot and salty — the cheese is the salt. |
| **Tostada rounds** | Sweet toasted corn under sharp salt. |
| **Avocado and lime** | Rich, green and mild against dry salt shards. |
| **Mango or pineapple with chili** | Fruit sugar and heat cutting a very salty cheese. |
| **Pickled red onion** | Acid and color. Also the prettiest bite on this page. |
| **Black bean dip** | Earthy and mild with salt crumbled over it. |
| **Mexican lager, margaritas, dry rosé** | Cold and acidic, which is what this much salt needs. |

**Skip it with:** pecorino, feta or any other salty cheese on the same board.
And skip delicate fresh cheeses next to it — cotija is loud.

## How to prep it for the board

1. **Crumble it into a small bowl**, don't cube it. It's a seasoning cheese and
   it should look like one.
2. **Serve under an ounce per person.** It's saltier than almost anything else
   on the board.
3. **Put it beside the acidic and sweet things** — lime, pickled onion, mango.
   That's where it earns its place.
4. **Temper briefly**, 15 minutes. It doesn't change much with temperature.
5. **Give it a small spoon** rather than a knife.
6. **Use it as a finishing element too** — crumbled over avocado, beans or
   roasted vegetables elsewhere on the board.
7. **Fix:** too salty on its own? That's expected. Serve it on something rather
   than beside it.

## What to buy

| Product | Where | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Cacique Cotija** | Most grocery stores, Latin dairy case | The most widely available US brand. Reliable. |
| **V&V Supremo Cotija** | Grocery, Latin markets | Widely stocked, slightly drier. |
| **Cotija añejo, wedge** | Latin markets, cheese counters | The aged version in a block rather than pre-crumbled. Much better. |
| **Local Latin market cotija** | Latin markets | Often better and cheaper. Ask for *añejo*. |
| **Queso fresco** | Latin dairy case | The mild fresh counterpart — see its own page. |
| **Ricotta salata** | Cheese counters | The Italian equivalent. Milder, similar role. |

**Label check:** buy the block or wedge rather than pre-crumbled where you can.
Pre-crumbled cotija is usually drier and often anti-caked.

**Skip:** using it where you want a mild cheese. That's queso fresco, and the
two aren't interchangeable despite sitting next to each other in the case.

## Quick FAQ

**Is cotija like parmesan?**
Similar role — hard, salty, crumbled over things — but cotija is saltier and
less complex. It's a seasoning cheese rather than a savory-depth one.

**Cotija or queso fresco?**
Cotija is aged, hard and very salty. Queso fresco is fresh, soft and mild.
Cotija seasons a bite; queso fresco cools one.

**Does it melt?**
No. It softens slightly and holds its shape.

**How much per person?**
Under an ounce. It's very salty.

**Block or crumbles?**
Block. Pre-crumbled is drier and often coated to stop clumping.
