## The short version

Spanish cured pork sausage coloured and flavoured almost entirely by smoked
paprika. Firm, brick-red and sliceable. **Mexican chorizo is a different
product** — raw, loose and made to be cooked — and buying it by mistake is the
single most common error in this category.

## What it is

Spanish chorizo is coarsely chopped pork mixed with *pimentón* — Spanish smoked
paprika — plus garlic and salt, cased and air-dried for weeks to months.
*Pimentón dulce* makes *chorizo dulce*; *pimentón picante* makes *chorizo
picante*. Mexican chorizo uses fresh chilies and vinegar and is sold raw.

**Taste:** smoky, sweet-peppery, garlicky, salty
**Texture:** firm, slightly chewy, oily at room temperature
**Look:** brick red to deep orange, coarse fat flecks, often tied in a horseshoe

Pimentón is the mechanism, and it isn't just seasoning. Spanish paprika is made
from peppers dried slowly over oak smoke, so it brings smoke, sweetness and
colour in one ingredient. That's why chorizo tastes smoked without ever seeing
a smokehouse, and why the fat runs orange.

## What it pairs with — and why

| Pair it with | Why it works |
|---|---|
| **Manchego** | The Spanish pairing. Nutty sheep's milk against smoke and paprika. |
| **Mahón or Idiazábal** | Spanish cheeses whose own paprika rind or smoke echoes the sausage. |
| **Marcona almonds** | Salt and fat, and both are Spanish staples. |
| **Green olives — gordal, manzanilla** | Brine cutting oily paprika fat. |
| **Membrillo (quince paste)** | Sweetness against smoke and salt. |
| **Pan con tomate** | Acid and oil under a rich sausage. |
| **Rioja, Tempranillo, cold cider** | Spanish reds, or Asturian cider for the acid. |

**Skip it with:** delicate fresh cheeses — paprika and garlic bury them. And
skip putting chorizo next to Idiazábal *and* smoked gouda; that's smoke three
times.

## How to prep it for the board

1. **Buy the Spanish cured kind.** If it's soft, raw-looking, in a plastic tube
   and refrigerated, it's Mexican and needs cooking.
2. **Slice thin, on a slight diagonal.** Chorizo is firm and a diagonal cut
   gives a bigger, better-looking oval.
3. **Temper 20 minutes** so the paprika oil softens.
4. **Serve 1–2 oz per person.**
5. **Peel the casing** unless it's clearly natural and papery.
6. **Expect orange fingers.** Put it where guests can reach a napkin — the oil
   transfers to everything.
7. **Fix:** hard and dry at the end of the chub? Trim the cut face; the interior
   is fine.

| **Chorizo Ibérico** | Spanish grocers, specialty | Made from Iberian pork. Richer and much better. |
| **Fermín chorizo** | Whole Foods, specialty | Excellent, widely imported. |
| **Chorizo de León or Cantimpalo** | Spanish specialty grocers | Regional styles worth trying. |
| **Mexican chorizo** | Any grocery, refrigerated | A raw cooking sausage. Not a board item. |
| **Salchichón** | Spanish grocers | The peppered, non-paprika Spanish salami — see its own page. |

**Label check:** *dulce* is mild, *picante* is hot. Look for pimentón high in
the ingredients and a firm, dry sausage — not a soft refrigerated tube.

**Skip:** Mexican chorizo for a board, and any "chorizo-style" sausage that
lists paprika rather than pimentón. The smoke is the point.

## Quick FAQ

**What's the difference between Spanish and Mexican chorizo?**
Spanish chorizo is cured, firm and sliceable, flavoured with smoked paprika.
Mexican chorizo is raw, soft and made with fresh chilies and vinegar — it has to
be cooked.

**Dulce or picante?**
*Dulce* is sweet-smoky and mild; *picante* has hot pimentón. Dulce is the safer
board choice.

**Why is the fat orange?**
Paprika is fat-soluble, so the colour leaches into the fat during curing.

**How much per person?**
1–2 oz as one of several meats.

**Can I eat the casing?**
Natural casing is edible but tough. Most people peel it.
