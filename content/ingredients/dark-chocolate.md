## The short version

Chocolate gives a board an ending — a corner people move to when they've had
enough salt. **70% cocoa** is the sweet spot: bitter enough to work with cheese,
not so bitter that guests wince. Break it into irregular shards rather than laying
out neat squares.

## What it is

Cocoa solids, cocoa butter and sugar, with cocoa percentage indicating how much of
the bar is cocoa-derived. **60–70%** is approachable; **70–80%** is the board
range; **85%+** is bitter and divisive. Single-origin bars vary enormously — Madagascar
is fruity and bright, Ecuador floral, Venezuelan nutty.

**Taste:** bitter, sweet, fruity to nutty depending on origin
**Texture:** hard snap, melting on the tongue
**Look:** glossy dark brown shards with a clean break

The mechanism is bitterness plus fat. Cocoa's bitter compounds do what tannin
does — they clear fat from the palate — while the cocoa butter melts at body
temperature and coats it again. That double action is why chocolate pairs so well
with blue cheese: the bitterness handles the salt, the fat carries it, and the
sugar softens the funk.

## What it pairs with — and why

| Pair it with | Why it works |
|---|---|
| **Gorgonzola and blue cheese** | Bitter and sweet against salt and funk. The pairing most guests haven't tried and remember afterwards. |
| **Dried cherries** | Tart fruit and bitter chocolate — the oldest confectionery pairing there is. |
| **Candied citrus** | Orange and chocolate. Both bitter, both sweet, both from the same tradition. |
| **Aged gouda** | Caramel and butterscotch in the cheese meeting cocoa's roast notes. |
| **Hazelnuts and toasted nuts** | Gianduja logic — chocolate and hazelnut share roasted aromatic compounds. |
| **Flaky sea salt** | Salt suppresses bitterness and amplifies cocoa. Sprinkle it on. |

**Skip it with:** cured meat and delicate fresh cheeses. Chocolate belongs in its
own corner.

## How to prep it for the board

1. **Break it into irregular shards**, not squares. Shards look generous; a grid
   of squares looks like a vending machine.
2. **Break it cold**, straight from the fridge, for a clean snap — then let it
   come to room temperature to serve.
3. **Put it in its own corner**, with the nuts, dried fruit and candied citrus.
4. **Serve at room temperature.** Cold chocolate tastes of almost nothing.
5. **Serve 0.5 oz per person.**
6. **Finish with flaky salt** on a few shards.
7. **Fix:** whitish bloom on the surface? That's cocoa butter, not mould. Harmless,
   and it still tastes fine.

## What to buy

| Product | Where | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **70% dark chocolate bar** | Any grocery | The board default. Lindt, Green & Black's, Ghirardelli all work. |
| **Single-origin bars** | Specialty, chocolatiers | Madagascar (fruity), Ecuador (floral), Venezuela (nutty). Worth exploring. |
| **Valrhona or Michel Cluizel** | Specialty, online | Reference-quality. Noticeably better. |
| **Sea salt dark chocolate** | Grocery | Already finished. A shortcut that works. |
| **Chocolate-covered nuts or citrus** | Chocolatiers, specialty | Two ingredients in one. See their own pages. |

**Label check:** cocoa or cocoa mass should be the first ingredient, not sugar.
Most dark chocolate contains soy lecithin and traces of dairy — worth flagging.

**Skip:** anything under 50% cocoa on a cheese board, and chocolate with fillings.

## Quick FAQ

**What percentage dark chocolate is best for a cheese board?**
70% — bitter enough to work with cheese, approachable enough that guests will eat
it.

**Why does chocolate go with blue cheese?**
Cocoa's bitterness clears the fat and the sugar softens the salt and funk, while
the cocoa butter carries it all.

**What's the white film on my chocolate?**
Cocoa butter bloom, caused by temperature change. Harmless, and the chocolate is
fine to eat.

**Should chocolate be served cold?**
No — room temperature. Cold chocolate is muted and doesn't melt properly on the
tongue.

**How much per person?**
About half an ounce — two or three shards.
