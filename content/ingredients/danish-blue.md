## The short version

*Danablu* — a Danish cow's-milk blue created in the 1920s as an affordable
answer to Roquefort. Salty, sharp and direct, with none of Roquefort's
complexity and about a quarter of the price. It's the workhorse blue, and
that's a legitimate role.

## What it is

Danish Blue is made from homogenized cow's milk, which is unusual and matters —
homogenization breaks up fat globules, making the paste whiter and the texture
softer and more uniform. It's aged eight to twelve weeks and heavily veined.

**Taste:** salty, sharp, tangy, straightforward, short finish
**Texture:** soft, moist, crumbly, spreadable when warm
**Look:** very white paste with dense blue-green veining

Homogenization is the mechanism, and it explains both the strengths and the
limits. Breaking up the fat exposes more surface to lipase enzymes, so flavor
develops fast — which is how a two-month cheese tastes this sharp. It also
means the flavor is one-dimensional: intensity without the layers a raw-milk
blue develops over time.

## What it pairs with — and why

| Pair it with | Why it works |
|---|---|
| **Honey** | Sweet against salt. Non-negotiable with a blue this direct. |
| **Rye crispbread** | Sour, earthy rye against sharp salt. Danish cheese, Danish bread. |
| **Pear and apple** | Cool, sweet, acidic relief. |
| **Walnuts** | Tannin meets blue-mold bitterness, and the crunch breaks up a soft paste. |
| **Fig jam** | Dense sweetness with enough body to hold. |
| **Celery and endive** | Bitter crunch against a soft salty paste. |
| **Port, sweet sherry, dark beer** | Sugar or malt against salt. |

**Skip it with:** delicate fresh cheeses, smoked cheeses, and other blues.
Danish Blue next to Stilton or Roquefort reads as the cheap one, which it is —
but on its own board it does the job perfectly well.

## How to prep it for the board

1. **Temper 45 minutes.** Cold, it's mostly salt; warm, it softens toward
   spreadable and improves considerably.
2. **Crumble it rather than slicing.** It's soft and moist and it doesn't hold
   a clean cut.
3. **Serve three-quarters to an ounce per person.**
4. **Give it its own knife.**
5. **Put honey beside it.** This cheese is sharp without much complexity, and
   sweetness is what makes it interesting.
6. **Use it for cooking as well** — it melts into dressings and sauces better
   than firmer blues, which makes a wedge go further.
7. **Fix:** browning at the cut face is oxidation. Trim thinly.

| **Castello Creamy Blue** | Grocery cheese case | Softer and milder. A good entry point for nervous guests. |
| **Saint Agur** | Grocery cheese case, Whole Foods | French, creamy, mild and widely liked. A better board blue at a small premium. |
| **Gorgonzola dolce** | Cheese counters, grocery | The gentler Italian option — see its own page. |
| **Stilton** | Whole Foods | The step up in complexity — see its own page. |

**Label check:** "Danablu" or a PGI mark for the genuine Danish product. Much
of what's sold as generic "blue cheese" in the US is domestic and often
sharper.

**Skip:** pre-crumbled blue cheese in a tub for board use. It's dried and often
anti-caked, and it looks like salad topping.

## Quick FAQ

**Is Danish Blue a good cheese board blue?**
Yes, as the budget option. It's sharp and direct rather than complex, and it
needs honey beside it — but it does the job.

**Why is it so white?**
The milk is homogenized, which breaks up the fat and lightens the paste. It
also speeds up flavor development.

**Danish Blue or Gorgonzola?**
Gorgonzola dolce is gentler and creamier. Danish Blue is sharper and cheaper.
For a nervous crowd, Gorgonzola.

**What's Saint Agur?**
A French creamy blue, mild and widely liked. A good step up from Danish Blue
for a small premium.

**How much per person?**
Under an ounce.
