## The short version

You already have rosemary and thyme on the savoury half of the board. Mint is the
one for the other half. It goes with melon, berries, feta and yogurt, it costs
about a dollar, and it is the only garnish that people actually eat rather than
push aside.

## What it is

Spearmint, usually — the sweet, mild mint sold in every supermarket. Peppermint is
sharper and much more menthol-forward, and is better in a drink than on a plate.
Both are sold in bunches and both wilt fast.

**Taste:** sweet and cooling, grassy, with a clean menthol finish
**Texture:** soft leaves that bruise easily
**Look:** bright mid-green, slightly crinkled

The mechanism is a temperature illusion. Menthol binds to the receptor that
reports cold, so mint literally makes the mouth register a drop in temperature
that is not happening. On a board loaded with fat and salt, that false coolness
acts like a palate cleanser without needing anything cold on the table.

## What it pairs with — and why

| Pair it with | Why it works |
|---|---|
| **Melon** | Sweet water and cooling herb. With prosciutto over the top it becomes a whole course. |
| **Berries** | Acid, sugar and menthol. Torn mint through a bowl of berries is a two-minute upgrade. |
| **Feta** | The Greek plate. Salt and brine against sweet herb, and both are cheap. |
| **Labneh** | Cool on cool. Mint, olive oil and labneh is a mezze standard for a reason. |
| **Medjool dates** | Very sweet and very soft. Mint is the only thing that cuts them without adding salt. |
| **Fresh mozzarella** | Where basil is the default, mint is the more interesting choice with fruit alongside. |

**Skip it with:** blue cheese, washed-rind cheese and dry-cured ham. Mint fights
all three.

## How to prep it for the board

1. **Buy it the same day** if you can. It wilts faster than any other herb here.
2. **Use whole sprigs** on the board and picked leaves in the fruit.
3. **Tear rather than chop.** A knife bruises the leaves and turns the edges black.
4. **Slap a sprig between your palms** before laying it down. It releases the oils
   and the board smells of mint as it arrives.
5. **A sprig per person** is plenty.
6. **Keep the bunch in water** until the last minute.
7. **Fix:** already limp? Ten minutes in ice water revives it almost completely.

## What to buy

| Product | Where | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Spearmint bunch** | Any grocery, produce section | The default. Sweet and mild. Buy the brightest bunch. |
| **Living mint plant** | Supermarkets, garden centres | Costs the same as two bunches and lasts months on a windowsill. |
| **Moroccan mint** | Middle Eastern grocers | Sweeter and more aromatic. The best version for fruit and mezze. |
| **Chocolate mint** | Farmers markets, garden centres | Genuinely tastes faintly of chocolate. Excellent on a dessert board. |
| **Peppermint** | Grocery, herb section | Sharper and cooler. Better in drinks than beside cheese. |

**Label check:** buy by eye. Leaves should be firm and unbruised, stems should not
be slimy.

**Skip:** dried mint for garnish. It is a useful cooking herb and a bad-looking
board ingredient.

## Quick FAQ

**Does mint belong on a charcuterie board?**
On the fruit and mezze parts of one, yes. Beside cured meat and aged cheese,
rosemary or thyme is the better herb.

**Which mint should I buy?**
Spearmint is the standard sweet mint and the one to use. Peppermint is sharper and
more medicinal.

**How do I keep it from wilting?**
Stand the bunch in a glass of water like flowers, and put whole sprigs on the board
rather than picked leaves.

**What does mint go with on a board?**
Melon, berries, feta, labneh and dates. Anything sweet, watery or dairy.

**How much per person?**
A sprig. It is a garnish and an aroma.
