## The short version

You're serving the seeds — *arils* — not the fruit. Scattered across a finished
board they look like garnets and add sharp bursts of acid. The only real
difficulty is opening one without redecorating the kitchen. Do it in a bowl of
water.

## What it is

A pomegranate is a leathery-skinned fruit packed with hundreds of arils: a
crunchy seed inside a sac of tart red juice, separated by bitter white
membrane. Peak season is October to January, which makes it the winter board
fruit.

**Taste:** sharply tart, sweet, faintly tannic
**Texture:** a burst of juice, then a small crunchy seed
**Look:** translucent ruby beads — the most jewel-like thing available

Scale is the mechanism, and it's why pomegranate works where other fruit
doesn't. Everything else on a board is measured in slices and clusters.
Pomegranate arils are tiny, so they scatter into the gaps between other
ingredients — filling a board visually and adding bursts of acid without taking
up a slot.

## What it pairs with — and why

| Pair it with | Why it works |
|---|---|
| **Fresh chèvre and feta** | Tart red beads on stark white cheese. The best-looking pairing in this section. |
| **Whipped feta or labneh** | Scattered over the top of a dip. Standard in Middle Eastern cooking. |
| **Blue cheese** | Sharp acid cutting salt and funk. |
| **Walnuts and pistachios** | Persian pairing — pomegranate, walnut and herbs go back centuries. |
| **Blood oranges** | Two winter fruits, both red, both acidic. |
| **Roasted vegetables and grain salads** | Worth knowing — the arils are as useful elsewhere on a table. |
| **Sparkling wine, rosé, Prosecco** | Bubbles scrub fat the way the seeds scrub the palate, and the wine's acid matches the fruit's rather than fighting it. |

**Skip it with:** nothing on flavour. The scatter is small enough that it never
dominates.

## How to prep it for the board

1. **Open it underwater.** Score the skin into quarters, submerge in a bowl of
   water, and break it apart with your hands. The arils sink, the white
   membrane floats. Skim the membrane, drain the arils. No spraying, no
   stained shirt.
2. **Skip the wooden-spoon method** — hitting a halved pomegranate over a bowl
   works but sprays juice everywhere.
3. **Dry the arils on a towel** before they go near cheese.
4. **Scatter, don't pile.** Spread them across the finished board so they land
   in the gaps.
5. **Serve one to two tablespoons per person.**
6. **Do it last**, after everything else is in place.
7. **Fix:** arils keep three to four days refrigerated in a sealed container, so
   this can be done the day before.

## What to buy

| Product | Where | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Whole pomegranates** | Grocery, Oct–Jan | Heavy for their size with taut skin. Cheapest by far. |
| **Pre-packed arils** | Grocery refrigerated section | Convenient and much more expensive. Check the date — they go fast. |
| **Wonderful variety** | Most US grocery | The standard American variety. Reliable. |
| **Middle Eastern markets** | Local | Often cheaper and better in season. |
| **Pomegranate molasses** | Middle Eastern markets, Whole Foods | The concentrated syrup. A different but useful board item. |
| **Dried barberries** | Persian markets | The tart red alternative when pomegranates aren't around. |

**Label check:** a whole pomegranate should feel heavy and have tight, slightly
angular skin. Round and light means it's drying out.

**Skip:** pre-packed arils if you have ten minutes. They cost several times as
much and don't taste as good.

## Quick FAQ

**How do I open a pomegranate without the mess?**
Score the skin, submerge it in a bowl of water, and break it apart underwater.
The arils sink and the membrane floats.

**Do you eat the seeds?**
Yes. The crunchy seed inside each juice sac is edible and part of the texture.

**How long do the arils keep?**
Three to four days refrigerated in a sealed container, so you can prep ahead.

**How much per person?**
One to two tablespoons, scattered rather than served in a pile.

**When are they in season?**
October through January — which makes them the winter board fruit.
