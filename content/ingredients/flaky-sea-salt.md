## The short version

The single highest-return thing in this whole section. A pinch of flaky salt over
burrata, over honey-drizzled cheese, over dark chocolate — it doesn't just make
things saltier, it makes them taste more like themselves. One box lasts a year and
costs less than a wedge of cheese.

## What it is

Sea salt crystallised into thin, hollow pyramid flakes rather than dense cubes.
**Maldon** is the reference; Halen Môn, Jacobsen and fleur de sel are the other
names to know. What matters is the shape — flakes dissolve slowly and crunch
audibly, which table salt never does.

**Taste:** clean salinity, no bitterness or metallic edge
**Texture:** crisp flakes that crunch, then dissolve
**Look:** white pyramid flakes, translucent at the edges

The mechanism is delayed dissolution. A dense salt crystal dissolves instantly and
you register uniform saltiness. A flake sits on the surface and dissolves as you
chew, so you get a burst of salt separate from the food it's on — which reads as
seasoning rather than as salted food. That contrast is why it works on sweet
things too.

## What it pairs with — and why

| Pair it with | Why it works |
|---|---|
| **Burrata and fresh mozzarella** | Both are almost unseasoned by design. Salt and olive oil are what turn them from an ingredient into a dish. |
| **Honey drizzled on cheese** | Salt suppresses the perception of sweetness slightly and sharpens everything around it. The best three-second upgrade on any board. |
| **Dark chocolate** | Salt on chocolate is a fixed pairing because it cuts bitterness and amplifies cocoa. |
| **Olive oil** | Oil, salt, bread. The Mediterranean's simplest plate. |
| **Sliced tomatoes and fruit** | Salt draws out sweetness and moisture. Try it on melon or peach. |
| **Caramel and dessert boards** | Salted caramel exists for the same reason. |

**Skip it with:** already very salty things — cured meat, aged cheese, olives,
anchovies. They don't need it.

## How to prep it for the board

1. **Put a small dish of it out** with the board, so guests can use it themselves.
2. **Pinch it between fingers** and scatter from six inches up — this distributes
   it evenly rather than dropping a clump.
3. **Finish the burrata yourself** before serving. It's the demonstration.
4. **Never grind it.** Flaky salt in a grinder is just salt.
5. **Add it last**, right before the board goes out. It dissolves on wet surfaces
   within minutes.
6. **Keep the box dry.** Humidity turns flakes into a solid brick.
7. **Fix:** clumped? Break it up with the back of a spoon. It still works.

## What to buy

| Product | Where | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Maldon sea salt flakes** | Any grocery, specialty | The reference. Large pyramid flakes, clean flavour. Buy the box. |
| **Fleur de sel** | French delis, specialty | Finer, moister, more mineral. The French finishing salt. |
| **Jacobsen or Halen Môn** | Specialty, online | Excellent regional producers. Worth trying. |
| **Smoked sea salt** | Specialty | Real smoke. Good on cheese and eggs, wrong on chocolate. |
| **Black lava or Himalayan pink salt** | Specialty | Mostly visual. Fine, but a Maldon flake is better on the palate. |

**Label check:** you want flakes, not fine grains. "Sea salt" in a shaker is not
the same product.

**Skip:** anything already ground, and anything with anti-caking agents.

## Quick FAQ

**Why use flaky salt instead of regular salt?**
The flakes dissolve slowly, so you get a distinct burst of salt and a crunch
rather than uniform saltiness. That contrast is what makes it a finishing salt.

**What do you put flaky salt on?**
Burrata, honey-drizzled cheese, dark chocolate, olive oil, and fresh fruit.

**Is Maldon worth it?**
Yes. It's a few dollars a box, lasts a year, and it's the most noticeable cheap
upgrade you can make to a board.

**Should I grind it?**
No — grinding destroys the flakes and the whole point of the product.

**How do I store it?**
Somewhere dry, with the lid on. Humidity turns flakes into a solid block.
