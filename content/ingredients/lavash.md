## The short version

A thin Armenian flatbread, traditionally baked against the wall of a clay
oven. Bought soft it wraps and folds; brushed with oil and baked ten minutes it
becomes a shattering cracker. One package gives you two different things on the
board, which nothing else here does.

## What it is

Lavash is one of the oldest continuously made breads in the world — flour,
water, salt, sometimes yeast, rolled paper-thin and slapped onto the hot wall
of a *tonir* oven. UNESCO lists its preparation as intangible cultural heritage
of Armenia. It's sold in the US both soft (in the bread aisle) and pre-crisped
as crackerbread.

**Taste:** plain, wheaty, faintly toasted
**Texture soft:** pliable, chewy, foldable
**Texture crisped:** brittle, blistered, shatters
**Look:** large thin sheets, blistered, often seeded

Versatility is the mechanism, and it's a practical one rather than a flavor
one. Every other base on a board arrives committed — a cracker is crisp, a
bread is soft. Lavash lets you decide, and lets you serve both from the same
package, which is genuinely useful when you're buying for a board and don't
know what the cheese lineup will need.

## What it pairs with — and why

| Pair it with | Why it works |
|---|---|
| **Hummus, labneh, muhammara** | Soft lavash tears and scoops; crisp lavash shatters into it. Both work. |
| **Feta, olives and cucumber** | The mezze plate. Wrap them in soft lavash or pile them on crisp shards. |
| **Fresh herbs and white cheese** | The Armenian and Persian way — a handful of tarragon and mint rolled in soft lavash with cheese. |
| **Prosciutto and soft cheese** | Soft lavash rolls and slices into pinwheels, which no cracker can do. |
| **Za'atar and olive oil** | Brush, scatter, bake. The crisped version becomes a finished snack. |
| **Grilled vegetables** | Soft lavash is the wrap; the board becomes assemble-your-own. |
| **Dry rosé, retsina, arak** | Eastern Mediterranean drinking. |

**Skip it with:** nothing in particular. Lavash is as neutral as matzo and more
flexible. The only mistake is buying soft lavash and letting it dry out
uncovered before you decide what to do with it.

## How to prep it for the board

1. **Decide which version you want, or do both.** Soft for wrapping and
   rolling, crisped for shards.
2. **To crisp it:** brush a sheet with olive oil, scatter salt and za'atar or
   sesame, bake at 350°F for 6–8 minutes until blistered and golden. Break by
   hand.
3. **To keep it soft:** store it sealed and don't unwrap until serving.
   Uncovered lavash goes stiff within twenty minutes.
4. **Roll pinwheels for the board:** spread soft lavash with herbed cream
   cheese, layer prosciutto or cucumber, roll tight, slice into rounds. Ten
   minutes, and it's the most "catered" thing on the board.
5. **Serve 2–3 pieces per person.**
6. **Fix:** stiffened soft lavash can be revived with a light spritz of water
   and 30 seconds in a warm pan — or just crisp it in the oven and change plan.

## Quick FAQ

**Is lavash the same as pita?**
No. Pita puffs and forms a pocket; lavash is thinner, flat, and has no pocket.

**Soft or crisp — which should I buy?**
Soft, if you're willing to spend ten minutes. You can crisp half and keep half
soft, which gives you two ingredients from one package.

**How do I crisp it?**
Brush with oil, salt it, and bake at 350°F for 6–8 minutes. Break by hand.

**How many per person?**
2–3 pieces of either version.

**Why does soft lavash go stiff so fast?**
It's extremely thin, so it loses moisture in minutes. Keep it sealed until you
serve it.
