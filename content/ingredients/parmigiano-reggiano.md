## The short version

The hard, crystalline, intensely savory cheese from Emilia-Romagna. Twelve
months minimum, usually twenty-four or more. Break it into rough shards with a
short blade — slicing it is both difficult and wrong.

## What it is

Parmigiano-Reggiano is a protected designation with rules that would exhaust
most producers: raw cow's milk from a defined zone, no additives, copper vats,
minimum twelve months' aging, and an inspection where each wheel is hammered
and either branded or rejected. The pin-dotted rind is stamped, not printed.

**Taste:** intensely savory, nutty, salty, faintly fruity
**Texture:** hard, granular, crystalline, fractures rather than cuts
**Look:** pale straw yellow, dry, with a thick natural rind

Free glutamates are the mechanism, and they're why this cheese tastes like
more than it is. Long aging breaks proteins down into free amino acids —
glutamate above all — which is the compound the tongue reads as savory depth.
Parmigiano is one of the most glutamate-dense foods in any cuisine, which is
why a small shard registers as a big flavor.

## What it pairs with — and why

| Pair it with | Why it works |
|---|---|
| **Aged balsamic vinegar** | The regional pairing — both come from Modena and Reggio. Sweet-sour syrup on salt crystals. |
| **Prosciutto di Parma** | Same province, same salt-and-umami register, different textures. |
| **Fresh pear** | Cool, wet, sweet against dry and salty. An Italian proverb tells farmers not to reveal how good it is. |
| **Grissini and taralli** | Plain dry bread against a very strong cheese — nothing competing. |
| **Walnuts and honey** | Bitter and sweet framing the salt. |
| **Lambrusco, Prosecco, Barolo** | Bubbles or tannin, both of which handle the salt. |
| **Aged rum, Vin Santo** | The dessert-course direction, and it works. |

**Skip it with:** parmesan crisps on the same board — that's the same flavor
twice, one of them worse — and delicate fresh cheeses, which lose completely.

## How to prep it for the board

1. **Break it, don't slice it.** Use a short almond-shaped parmesan knife, push
   it into the paste, and lever off rough shards. Slicing a hard cheese fights
   its structure and produces dust.
2. **Temper 30 minutes.** The fruity notes only appear at room temperature.
3. **Serve about 1 oz per person.** It's intense and people take small pieces.
4. **Leave the shards irregular** and pile them. Uniform cubes make an
   expensive cheese look cheap.
5. **Put balsamic beside it** with a small spoon. This is the pairing that
   justifies the price.
6. **Trim the hard outer rind** but keep it — it's the best thing you can drop
   into a soup or a pot of beans.
7. **Fix:** dried at the cut face? Trim a thin layer and wrap the rest in
   parchment, not plastic. Plastic suffocates it.

**Label check:** the pin-dot lettering on the rind reading PARMIGIANO-REGGIANO.
If it isn't there, it isn't the cheese. Anything labeled "parmesan" in the US
is legally something else.

**Skip:** pre-grated parmesan in a shaker, and anything labeled just "parmesan"
for board use. Different product, different price for a reason.

## Quick FAQ

**Parmesan vs. Parmigiano-Reggiano — is there a difference?**
Yes. Parmigiano-Reggiano is a protected Italian designation with strict rules.
"Parmesan" in the US is an unregulated name that can mean almost anything.

**How do I cut it?**
Break it with a short parmesan knife into rough shards. Don't slice.

**What are the crunchy crystals?**
Tyrosine crystals from long aging — the same as in aged cheddar and gouda.

**Can I eat the rind?**
It's edible but unpleasantly hard. Save it for soups and stocks instead.

**How much per person?**
About 1 oz. It's intense, and people take small shards.
