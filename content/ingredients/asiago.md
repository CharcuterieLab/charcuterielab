## The short version

Two entirely different cheeses under one DOP. *Asiago Pressato* is young, soft
and mild. *Asiago d'Allevo* is aged, hard and sharp. Buying the wrong one is
the most common mistake with this cheese, and the label rarely helps.

## What it is

Asiago comes from the Veneto, and the DOP covers both styles. Pressato is made
from whole milk and aged 20–40 days — pale, elastic and gentle. D'Allevo is
made from partly skimmed milk and aged four months to two years, ending up dry,
granular and assertive.

**Taste (fresh):** mild, milky, buttery, faintly sweet
**Taste (aged):** sharp, nutty, salty, savory
**Texture:** supple and springy fresh; hard and crumbly aged
**Look:** pale ivory with small holes fresh; deep straw and dry aged

Two products under one name is the real mechanism here, and it matters more
than any flavor note. If you want a mild slicing cheese, buy Pressato. If you
want a hard cheese to break into shards on a board, buy *d'Allevo* or a wheel
labeled *vecchio* or *stravecchio*. The word "Asiago" alone tells you almost
nothing.

## What it pairs with — and why

| Pair it with | Why it works |
|---|---|
| **Grissini and taralli** | Plain Italian bread against either version. |
| **Prosciutto and soppressata** | Salt and cure with a nutty cheese — better with the aged style. |
| **Honey and fig jam** | Sweet against sharp. Aged Asiago needs this; fresh doesn't. |
| **Apple and pear** | Fruit acid, especially good with the fresh version. |
| **Marinated olives and artichokes** | Briny and oily neighbors on an Italian board. |
| **Walnuts** | Tannin and oil against a salty granular cheese. |
| **Prosecco, Soave, Valpolicella** | Northern Italian wines, light to medium. |

**Skip it with:** delicate fresh cheeses if you've got the aged style. And
don't put Asiago and Parmigiano side by side — aged Asiago reads as a cheaper
version of the same idea.

## How to prep it for the board

1. **Know which one you bought.** Fresh slices, aged breaks. Treating aged
   Asiago like a slicing cheese produces crumbs.
2. **Fresh: cut into cubes or thin slices.** It's supple and holds a clean cut.
3. **Aged: break into shards** with a short parmesan knife.
4. **Temper 30 minutes** either way.
5. **Serve 1–2 oz per person.**
6. **Use it as the value hard cheese** on a big Italian board — it costs well
   under Parmigiano and does a similar job.
7. **Fix:** rubbery and bland? That's Pressato, and it needs salt and oil, or a
   sharper partner beside it.

## What to buy

| Product | Where | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Asiago d'Allevo (aged/vecchio)** | Cheese counters, Italian delis, Whole Foods | The board version. Sharp, granular, good value. |
| **Asiago Pressato (fresh)** | Grocery deli case | The mild slicing version. Fine, unremarkable. |
| **BelGioioso Asiago** | Most grocery stores | American-made, aged style, widely available and decent. |
| **Asiago Stravecchio** | Specialty shops, Italian delis | Two years plus. Genuinely excellent and much rarer. |
| **Grana Padano** | Grocery cheese case | The alternative if you want hard-and-Italian for the same money. |
| **Aged provolone** | Italian delis | Another sharp Italian option — see its own page. |

**Label check:** *d'Allevo*, *vecchio* or *stravecchio* for the aged style;
*pressato* for the fresh. If the label says nothing, ask — or assume it's the
mild one.

**Skip:** pre-shredded Asiago for a board, and "Asiago" bagels or seasoning as
a guide to what the cheese tastes like. They're modeled on the aged style.

## Quick FAQ

**What's the difference between fresh and aged Asiago?**
Fresh (Pressato) is soft, mild and springy. Aged (d'Allevo) is hard, sharp and
granular. Same DOP, completely different cheeses.

**Which one goes on a charcuterie board?**
The aged one, broken into shards.

**Is it like parmesan?**
Aged Asiago is in the same family — hard, granular, savory — but less complex
and cheaper.

**Can you eat the rind?**
No. Trim it.

**How much per person?**
1–2 oz.
