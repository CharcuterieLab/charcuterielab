## The short version

Where fig jam sweetens, apricot jam cuts. It keeps real acidity, which makes it
the better choice on a board that's already rich — lots of soft cheese, lots of
fatty cured meat. It's also the traditional glaze for baked brie, and cheap
enough that there's no reason not to have a jar.

## What it is

Apricots cooked with sugar and lemon. Sold as jam (with fruit pieces), preserve
(chunkier), or *confiture*. French apricot jam tends to be looser and tarter;
American versions are sweeter and more set.

**Taste:** sweet-tart, bright, faintly almond
**Texture:** looser than fig jam, with soft fruit pieces
**Look:** glowing orange, translucent

The mechanism is acid that survives cooking. Most fruit loses its edge under
sugar and heat; apricot's malic acid is strong enough to come through, which is
why apricot jam registers as a contrast to fat rather than just an addition to
it. That makes it the jam for boards that need cutting rather than sweetening.

## What it pairs with — and why

| Pair it with | Why it works |
|---|---|
| **Brie and soft-ripened cheese** | Acid slicing soft fat, where fig jam would only add sugar to it. Also the standard baked brie topping. |
| **Manchego** | Apricot and almond share aroma compounds, and manchego's nuttiness meets both. |
| **Fresh goat cheese** | Two acids in different registers — fruity against lactic — with sugar bridging them. |
| **Prosciutto** | Bright sweet-tart against salt. Cleaner than a fig jam on a cracker with ham. |
| **Aged gouda** | Caramel and butterscotch cut by fruit acid. |
| **Baked brie, with almonds** | The most-made cheese recipe in the world, and this is the jam it uses. |

**Skip it with:** blue cheese. The acid clashes with the ammoniated edge, and
blue wants the flat sweetness of fig or honey instead.

## How to prep it for the board

1. **Decant into a small bowl** with its own spreader.
2. **Stir it first** — apricot jam separates more than most.
3. **Serve 0.5 oz per person.**
4. **Put it beside the brie**, not the blue.
5. **Loosen with a teaspoon of water** if it's very set.
6. **Warm it slightly** if you're using it as a glaze rather than a spread.
7. **Fix:** too sweet? A squeeze of lemon brings the tartness back.

## What to buy

| Product | Where | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Bonne Maman apricot preserve** | Any grocery | The default. Widely available, good fruit content, not overly set. |
| **French confiture d'abricot** | French delis, specialty | Looser and tarter. The best version of this jam. |
| **Trader Joe's apricot preserves** | Trader Joe's | Cheap and perfectly good. |
| **Apricot and rosemary jam** | Farmers markets, specialty | Herb versions work well with manchego and hard cheese. |
| **Mostarda-style apricot** | Italian delis | Mustard-spiked. A different and excellent thing — see mostarda. |

**Label check:** apricots first, sugar second. Anything led by sugar or glucose
syrup will be sweet and thin.

**Skip:** apricot jelly with no fruit pieces. The texture is half of what jam is
doing on a board.

## Quick FAQ

**Is apricot jam better than fig jam for a cheese board?**
They do different jobs. Fig jam sweetens and suits blue cheese; apricot jam is
tarter and cuts rich soft cheeses better.

**What cheese goes with apricot jam?**
Brie and soft-ripened cheeses, manchego, fresh goat cheese, and aged gouda. Not
blue.

**What jam do I use for baked brie?**
Apricot is the traditional choice, usually with almonds.

**Jam or preserve — does it matter?**
Preserve has larger fruit pieces. On a board the extra texture is a small plus.

**How much per person?**
About half an ounce. One small bowl covers a board.
