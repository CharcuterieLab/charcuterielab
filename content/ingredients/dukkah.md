## The short version

A coarse Egyptian blend of toasted nuts, sesame, coriander and cumin. The
traditional service is a bowl of dukkah beside a bowl of olive oil: you dip bread
in the oil, then in the dukkah. On a board it's the thing people ask about, and it
costs almost nothing.

## What it is

Toasted hazelnuts or almonds, sesame seeds, coriander seed, cumin and salt,
pounded to a coarse rubble — never a powder. Recipes vary by family. Some add
fennel, black pepper, mint or chickpeas.

**Taste:** nutty, warm, earthy, citrusy from the coriander
**Texture:** coarse and gritty, deliberately uneven
**Look:** sandy brown rubble flecked with sesame

The mechanism is aroma in a dry form. Wet condiments — jam, honey, mustard —
add flavour but also moisture, which is why they need their own dish and a knife.
Dukkah delivers as much aroma with no moisture at all, so it can be sprinkled
directly over cheese without softening anything it touches.

## What it pairs with — and why

| Pair it with | Why it works |
|---|---|
| **Burrata** | Sprinkle it over the top with olive oil. Cream, spice, crunch — the best three-ingredient plate on any board. |
| **Labneh and thick yoghurt** | The traditional partner. Spice and grit against thick tang. |
| **Fresh goat cheese** | Roll a log in dukkah. Herbaceous, crunchy, and it looks deliberate. |
| **Olive oil and bread** | The original service. A bowl of each, and good bread. |
| **Pita chips and flatbread** | Dip in oil, then dukkah. The whole point. |
| **Soft-boiled eggs, if the board is a brunch board** | A standard Middle Eastern breakfast. |

**Skip it with:** delicate aged cheeses. Cumin and coriander will cover them.

## How to prep it for the board

1. **Make it:** toast 1/2 cup hazelnuts, 1/4 cup sesame seeds, 2 tbsp coriander
   seed and 1 tbsp cumin seed separately, since they toast at different rates.
2. **Pound or pulse coarsely.** Stop while it's still rubble — a powder is a
   different, worse thing.
3. **Salt generously** and add black pepper.
4. **Serve in a small bowl beside a bowl of good olive oil.**
5. **Or sprinkle it directly** over burrata, labneh, or a goat cheese log.
6. **Serve 0.25 oz per person.**
7. **Fix:** tastes dusty? Under-toasted spices. Toast until fragrant, not just warm.

## What to buy

| Product | Where | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Homemade dukkah** | Make it | Ten minutes and far better than any jar. Toast everything separately. |
| **Trader Joe's dukkah** | Trader Joe's | Widely available and genuinely good. The easiest entry point. |
| **Middle Eastern market dukkah** | Middle Eastern grocers | Often the best, and cheap. Varies by shop. |
| **Za'atar** | Middle Eastern markets, grocery | Related but different — thyme, sumac, sesame, no nuts. Also excellent on a board. |
| **Egyptian dukkah with chickpeas** | Specialty, online | Traditional variant. Lighter and crunchier. |

**Label check:** the nuts should be listed early. A dukkah that's mostly sesame
and spice is cheaper and much less interesting.

**Skip:** anything ground fine. Texture is half the ingredient.

## Quick FAQ

**What is dukkah?**
An Egyptian blend of toasted nuts, sesame seeds and spices, pounded coarse. It's
traditionally eaten by dipping bread in olive oil and then in the dukkah.

**How do I serve dukkah on a charcuterie board?**
A bowl of dukkah beside a bowl of olive oil with bread, or sprinkled directly over
burrata, labneh, or a goat cheese log.

**Is dukkah the same as za'atar?**
No. Za'atar is thyme, sumac and sesame with no nuts. Dukkah is nut-based and
coarser.

**Can I make dukkah at home?**
Yes, in about ten minutes. Toast the nuts and spices separately, then pound them
coarse with salt.

**Does dukkah contain allergens?**
Yes — tree nuts and sesame, both common allergens. Say so when you put it out.
