## The short version

Onions cooked slowly until they collapse into a dark sweet paste, then finished
with vinegar and sugar. Also sold as onion confit or *confiture d'oignons*. It
does what fig jam does — supplies sweetness — but from the savoury side, which
means it can sit next to meat without turning the board into dessert.

## What it is

Sliced onions cooked for an hour or more until deeply caramelised, then reduced
with red wine, balsamic or sherry vinegar and a little sugar. Red onions give a
darker, sweeter result; yellow onions a more savoury one.

**Taste:** deeply sweet, tangy, savoury, faintly winey
**Texture:** soft strands in a thick jammy base
**Look:** dark mahogany brown, glossy, visibly stranded

The mechanism is caramelisation plus acid. Long cooking converts the onions'
sugars into complex brown compounds — the same reaction that makes toast and
seared meat taste the way they do — and the vinegar keeps that sweetness from
being flat. So you get depth and brightness in one spoon, which no fruit jam
manages.

## What it pairs with — and why

| Pair it with | Why it works |
|---|---|
| **Aged cheddar** | Cheese and onion, elevated. Sharp acid against deep caramel, and both are assertive enough to hold. |
| **Gruyère and comté** | The French onion soup pairing, without the soup. Nutty alpine cheese and sweet onion belong together. |
| **Brie** | Soft fat with savoury sweetness — a better match than a fruit jam on a very ripe wheel. |
| **Saucisson sec and salami** | Fatty cured pork with vinegar-cut sweetness. |
| **Pâté and terrines** | Rich liver against dark sweet onion is a French bistro standard. |
| **Baguette and crostini** | The base. Spread thin, add cheese, done. |

**Skip it with:** delicate fresh cheeses. Onion jam buries anything mild.

## How to prep it for the board

1. **Decant into a small bowl** with a spreader.
2. **Serve 0.5 oz per person.**
3. **Put it beside the cheddar and the alpine cheese**, where it does the most.
4. **Make it:** slice 4 large onions, cook in butter or oil over low heat for
   45–60 minutes, then add 1/4 cup balsamic or red wine vinegar and 2 tbsp brown
   sugar and reduce until jammy.
5. **Serve at room temperature.** Cold from the fridge it's stiff and dull.
6. **Use it as the savoury sweet element** when the board already has fruit.
7. **Fix:** too sweet? More vinegar, a teaspoon at a time.

## What to buy

| Product | Where | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Confiture d'oignons** | French delis, specialty | The traditional version. Deep and wine-forward. |
| **Stonewall Kitchen onion jam** | Grocery, specialty | Widely available and reliable. |
| **Balsamic onion jam** | Cheese shops, farmers markets | Sharper and darker. Very good with cheddar. |
| **Cipollini onion confit** | Italian delis | Made with whole small onions. Chunkier and excellent. |
| **Homemade** | Make it | An hour of mostly unattended cooking, and it keeps two weeks. |

**Label check:** onions first, then vinegar and sugar. Anything led by sugar is a
sweet jelly with onions in it.

**Skip:** French onion dip. Completely different product.

## Quick FAQ

**What is onion jam?**
Caramelised onions cooked down with vinegar and sugar into a thick sweet-savoury
spread. Also called onion confit or confiture d'oignons.

**What cheese goes with onion jam?**
Aged cheddar above all, plus gruyère, comté, and brie.

**Can I make onion jam at home?**
Yes. Slice four onions, cook slowly in fat for about an hour, then reduce with
vinegar and brown sugar until jammy.

**How is it different from fig jam?**
It supplies the same sweetness from the savoury side, so it works next to meat
without making the board taste like dessert.

**How long does it keep?**
Two weeks homemade in the fridge; months unopened if bought.
