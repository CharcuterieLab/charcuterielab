## The short version

A firm Swiss alpine cheese aged five months to two years. Salty, nutty and
distinctly brothy — the savory note people describe as "meaty." It's the cheese
in fondue and in a croque monsieur, and it's just as good uncooked.

## What it is

Gruyère AOP comes from western Switzerland, made in wheels of about 35kg from
raw cow's milk. It's a cooked, pressed cheese — the curd is heated in copper
vats before pressing — which drives out moisture and produces a dense paste
that melts smoothly rather than separating.

**Taste:** salty, nutty, brothy, faintly sweet, more savory with age
**Texture:** firm and dense, supple, crystalline in older wheels
**Look:** pale gold, natural brownish rind, occasional small eyes

Cooked curd is the mechanism, and it explains both of Gruyère's qualities.
Heating the curd builds a tight protein structure with low moisture, which
gives the cheese its density and its long aging potential — and because that
structure holds together under heat, Gruyère melts into a smooth sauce instead
of splitting. Very few cheeses are excellent both raw and melted; this is one.

## What it pairs with — and why

| Pair it with | Why it works |
|---|---|
| **Baguette and cornichons** | The alpine cheese plate. Plain bread and sharp acid. |
| **Cured ham and speck** | Salt and smoke against a brothy savory cheese. |
| **Apple and pear** | Fruit acid cutting salt and fat — the standard alpine partner. |
| **Walnuts** | Tannic bitterness against nutty, salty density. |
| **Whole grain mustard and pickled onion** | Sharp and vinegary, and traditional. |
| **Dry Riesling, Chasselas, Chardonnay** | Swiss and Alsatian whites, both high-acid. |
| **Dark beer, dry cider** | Malt and acid both work. |

**Skip it with:** delicate fresh cheeses in the same corner, and heavy sweet
jams — Gruyère's salt and sweetness fight rather than balance.

## How to prep it for the board

1. **Cut into thick batons or rough chunks.** Its density suits a substantial
   piece; thin slices go waxy.
2. **Temper 30 minutes.**
3. **Serve 1–2 oz per person.**
4. **Buy the older one if you're choosing.** Twelve months and up develops the
   crystals and the brothy depth that make it interesting rather than merely
   good.
5. **Put it beside Comté** if you have both — same family, clearly different,
   and comparison boards are more fun than survey boards.
6. **Trim the rind.**
7. **Fix:** wrap in parchment. Plastic makes alpine cheeses sweat and turn
   sour at the surface.

## What to buy

| Product | Where | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Gruyère AOP, 12 months** | Whole Foods, cheese counters, most grocery | The standard and the right default. |
| **Le Gruyère Réserve (18+ months)** | Cheese counters, specialty | Crystalline and much deeper. The board version. |
| **Gruyère d'Alpage** | Specialty shops | Made in summer mountain huts. Rare, expensive, remarkable. |
| **Emmi Gruyère** | Grocery cheese case | Widely distributed Swiss brand. Reliable. |
| **Costco Gruyère** | Costco | Good price for a large board. |
| **Comté or Beaufort** | Cheese counters | The French alpine cousins — see their own pages. |

**Label check:** "Le Gruyère AOP" and Swiss origin. French Gruyère exists and
is legally distinct — it has holes and is milder. American "gruyere" is usually
something else entirely.

**Skip:** pre-shredded Gruyère for a board. It's coated with anti-caking starch
and it dries fast.

## Quick FAQ

**Does Gruyère have holes?**
Swiss Gruyère AOP has very few or none. French Gruyère does. If you're seeing
lots of holes, you're probably looking at Emmental.

**Gruyère or Comté?**
Gruyère is saltier and more directly savory; Comté is fruitier and more varied.
Both are excellent — put them side by side.

**Can you eat the rind?**
Trim it. It's natural but tough.

**Why is it in every fondue recipe?**
Cooked curd gives it a tight protein structure that melts smoothly instead of
splitting.

**How much per person?**
1–2 oz as one of three cheeses.
