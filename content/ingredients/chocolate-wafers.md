## The short version

Thin dark cocoa biscuits — the kind used to build icebox cakes. They're only
lightly sweet, and their bitterness is the point: on a board where every other
base is pale and neutral, a dark cocoa wafer is both a visual contrast and the
only base that brings its own edge.

## What it is

A thin crisp biscuit made with cocoa powder and relatively little sugar. Nabisco's
Famous Chocolate Wafers were the reference for decades; European chocolate
*biscuits secs* and dark digestive-style biscuits fill the same role. Thin and
snapping, not soft.

**Taste:** cocoa-bitter, lightly sweet, faintly salty
**Texture:** thin and crisp, snaps cleanly
**Look:** near-black rounds, matte

The mechanism is bitterness in a base. Every other sweet base on a board — graham,
shortbread, speculoos — contributes sugar and nothing that cuts. Cocoa's bitter
compounds clear fat the way tannin does, so a chocolate wafer under mascarpone
actually resets the palate rather than compounding the richness.

## What it pairs with — and why

| Pair it with | Why it works |
|---|---|
| **Mascarpone** | Bitter cocoa against sweet cream — the tiramisu relationship, in two components. |
| **Gorgonzola and blue cheese** | Cocoa bitterness handles the fat, the light sugar handles the salt. Unexpected and one of the best pairings in this group. |
| **Berries** | Dark base, red fruit, white cream. The best-looking sweet corner you can build. |
| **Dark chocolate** | Same corner, same flavour, different texture. |
| **Brandied cherries** | Black forest logic — cherry, chocolate, cream. |
| **Espresso and dessert wine** | Bitter coffee and bitter cocoa reinforce each other, and a sweet wine supplies the sugar the wafer withholds. |

**Skip it with:** savoury cheese and cured meat. Cocoa is unmistakably dessert.

## How to prep it for the board

1. **Fan them near the sweet corner**, where the dark colour reads as deliberate.
2. **Serve them whole.** They're already thin and small.
3. **Crumble a few** over mascarpone or ricotta — that's the best use.
4. **Serve 0.5 oz per person** — two or three wafers.
5. **Keep them dry.** Thin biscuits soften fast next to soft cheese.
6. **Use them for contrast.** On a board of cream and tan, this is the only black
   thing.
7. **Fix:** soft? 300°F for four minutes and cool completely.

## What to buy

| Product | Where | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Thin chocolate wafers** | Grocery, baking aisle | The icebox-cake biscuit. Barely sweet and ideal. |
| **Dark chocolate digestives** | British section, grocery | Thicker and coated on one side. Excellent with blue cheese. |
| **Chocolate biscuits secs** | European delis | Thin, dark, lightly sweet. The best version. |
| **Cocoa nib crackers** | Specialty, cheese shops | Savoury-leaning with real cocoa nibs. A genuine cheese cracker. |
| **Chocolate-dipped shortbread** | Grocery, specialty | Richer, and better beside coffee than under cheese. |

**Label check:** cocoa should be prominent and sugar shouldn't lead. A sweet
chocolate cookie is a different product and won't pair.

**Skip:** cream-filled chocolate sandwich cookies. Too sweet, and the filling gets
everywhere.

## Quick FAQ

**Do chocolate wafers go with cheese?**
Yes — mascarpone above all, and surprisingly well with blue cheese, where the
cocoa bitterness cuts the fat.

**What kind should I buy?**
Thin and barely sweet — the kind sold for icebox cakes, or European dark chocolate
biscuits. Not a sweet chocolate cookie.

**Can I use chocolate digestives instead?**
Yes, and they're excellent with blue cheese. They're thicker, so they carry more.

**Why put a dark base on a board?**
Contrast. Everything else on a board is cream, tan or brown, and a black wafer is
the only visual break available in a base.

**How many per person?**
Two or three, about half an ounce.
