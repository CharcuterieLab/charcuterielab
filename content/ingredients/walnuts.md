## The short version

Walnuts are the one nut with real bitterness, and that's the whole reason to use
them. Blue cheese, pears and figs all want something astringent alongside, and a
walnut is it. Toast them for ten minutes before they go anywhere near a board —
the difference is not subtle.

## What it is

The kernel of the walnut, sold as halves or pieces, with a thin papery skin that
carries most of the tannin. English (Persian) walnuts are the standard. Black
walnuts are a different, much stronger species — intense, oily and divisive.

**Taste:** buttery interior, bitter tannic skin
**Texture:** soft-crisp, oily, easily broken
**Look:** pale brown convoluted halves

The mechanism is tannin. Walnut skins carry astringent compounds that behave like
red wine in the mouth — they grip and clear fat in a way salt and acid don't. That
is exactly what a wedge of roquefort or a slice of ripe pear needs, and why the
pairing appears in every French kitchen.

## What it pairs with — and why

| Pair it with | Why it works |
|---|---|
| **Roquefort and gorgonzola** | The definitive pairing. Tannin against salt and fat; each one makes the other less overwhelming. |
| **Stilton** | Same logic, with the walnut supplying the crunch the cheese lacks. |
| **Pears** | Soft sweet fruit and bitter crunchy nut — the oldest cheese-board trio, with blue cheese. |
| **Dried figs** | Fig and walnut is a fixed pairing in bread across the Mediterranean. |
| **Aged goat cheese** | Sharp and dry against oily and bitter. |
| **Honey** | A drizzle over toasted walnuts and blue cheese is a complete dish in three ingredients. |

**Skip it with:** delicate fresh cheeses. Walnut bitterness flattens mozzarella
and burrata.

## How to prep it for the board

1. **Toast them.** 350°F for 8–10 minutes, tossed once. This is the single most
   important step on this page.
2. **Rub off the loose skin** in a towel while warm if you want less bitterness.
   Leave it on if you want more.
3. **Salt lightly while warm** if the board is savoury.
4. **Use halves, not pieces**, on the board — pieces look like baking supplies.
5. **Serve 0.5–1 oz per person.**
6. **Buy them in small quantities.** Walnuts are the fastest nut to go rancid.
7. **Fix:** bitter to the point of unpleasant? Toast longer, rub the skins off,
   or blanch them in boiling water for a minute first.

## What to buy

| Product | Where | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Raw walnut halves** | Any grocery, Costco | Buy raw and toast them yourself. Halves, not pieces. |
| **Californian walnuts** | Grocery | The standard. Mild and reliable. |
| **French Grenoble walnuts** | Specialty, online | Sweeter and less bitter. Sold in shell in autumn. |
| **In-shell walnuts with a nutcracker** | Grocery, autumn | Put them on the board in shell — it's theatre, and they're always fresh. |
| **Candied walnuts** | Grocery, make them | Sweet version. A different ingredient with different partners. |
| **Black walnuts** | Farmers markets, online | Intense, almost medicinal. For people who already know they like them. |

**Label check:** smell them. Rancid walnuts smell like paint, and it's the most
common problem with this nut. Buy from a shop with turnover, and refrigerate.

**Skip:** walnut pieces for board use, and any bag that smells at all sharp.

## Quick FAQ

**Should I toast walnuts for a charcuterie board?**
Yes. 350°F for 8–10 minutes. Untoasted walnuts taste flat and slightly stale.

**Why are my walnuts bitter?**
The papery skin carries tannins. Rub the skins off in a towel after toasting, or
blanch briefly, to soften the bitterness.

**What cheese goes with walnuts?**
Blue cheeses above all — roquefort, gorgonzola, stilton — plus aged goat cheese.

**How do I stop walnuts going rancid?**
Buy small quantities and keep them in the freezer. Walnuts spoil faster than any
other common nut.

**Halves or pieces?**
Halves. Pieces look like an ingredient rather than a serving.
