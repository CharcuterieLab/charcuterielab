## The short version

Citrus peel simmered in syrup until it turns translucent. Good candied orange is
chewy, bitter-sweet, and faintly floral — nothing like the neon cubes in
fruitcake mix. It's a dessert-corner ingredient: put it near chocolate,
mascarpone and blue cheese, not in the middle of the board.

## What it is

Strips or slices of citrus peel (or whole thin wheels) cooked slowly in sugar
syrup so the sugar replaces the water. Orange is the standard; lemon, citron and
grapefruit all exist. Sicilian and Calabrian producers make the best of it.

**Taste:** sweet, bitter, floral, intensely citrus
**Texture:** chewy and slightly firm, sometimes sugar-crusted
**Look:** translucent amber strips or glowing wheels

The mechanism is bitterness carried in sugar. Bitterness is the missing note on
most boards, and pith is where citrus keeps it. Candying preserves that
bitterness while making it edible, which is why candied peel can stand next to
blue cheese and dark chocolate where fresh orange would be flattened.

## What it pairs with — and why

| Pair it with | Why it works |
|---|---|
| **Gorgonzola and blue cheese** | Sugar handles the salt, bitterness handles the fat. Better than a date for the same job. |
| **Mascarpone** | Rich and flat on its own; candied orange gives it aroma and bite. |
| **Fresh ricotta** | Chop it fine over ricotta with honey — a Sicilian dessert on a board. |
| **Aged gouda** | Caramel and citrus, both concentrated. |
| **Dark chocolate** | The oldest pairing in the confectionery book, and it holds. |
| **Almonds and pistachios** | Sicilian again: citrus, nut, sugar. |

**Skip it with:** cured meat. Candied citrus is a dessert ingredient and it
doesn't sit well against salami.

## How to prep it for the board

1. **Snip into shorter pieces** if the strips are long — an inch is plenty.
2. **Fan them out** or set them in a small dish in the board's sweet corner.
3. **Group it with chocolate, nuts and soft sweet cheese**, away from the meat.
4. **Chop it finely** if you're scattering it over ricotta or mascarpone.
5. **Serve 0.5 oz per person.** It's very sweet.
6. **Buy whole wheels** if presentation matters — candied blood orange slices are
   spectacular.
7. **Fix:** hard and crystallised? Warm briefly in a low oven or steam for a
   minute; it softens.

## What to buy

| Product | Where | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Italian candied orange peel (scorza d'arancia)** | Italian delis, Eataly, online | The good stuff. Chewy, bitter, not overly sugared. |
| **Candied blood orange wheels** | Specialty, online | Whole translucent red discs. The best-looking thing in this category. |
| **Candied lemon peel** | Italian delis | Sharper and more floral. Excellent with ricotta. |
| **Candied citron (cedro)** | Italian delis | Firm, mild, mostly pith. Traditional and a bit of an acquired taste. |
| **Chocolate-dipped orange peel** | Chocolatiers, specialty | Dessert-ready. Put it out as-is. |

**Label check:** the ingredients should be citrus peel, sugar, glucose. Avoid
anything with artificial colour — that's the fruitcake mix.

**Skip:** diced "candied fruit mix" in tubs. Different product, and not a good one.

## Quick FAQ

**Is candied citrus the same as fruitcake mix?**
No. Fruitcake mix is dyed, diced, and mostly flavourless. Good candied peel is
translucent amber and tastes strongly of citrus.

**What goes with candied orange peel?**
Blue cheese, mascarpone, ricotta, dark chocolate, and almonds.

**Where does it go on a board?**
The dessert corner, with chocolate and nuts — away from the cured meat.

**How much per person?**
About half an ounce. It's very sweet and very concentrated.

**Can I make it myself?**
Yes — peel simmered in sugar syrup for an hour or two, then dried. It takes time
but almost no skill.
