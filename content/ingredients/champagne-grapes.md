## The short version

Tiny seedless grapes, each about the size of a peppercorn, growing in dense little
bunches. They are the same fruit that becomes a dried currant. On a board they do
something no other fruit does: a single small bunch draped over the edge of a
cheese makes the whole arrangement look composed, and it takes four seconds. The
season is short, so buy them when you see them.

## What it is

Zante currants, a very small seedless grape variety. Sold in loose small bunches in
late summer. The name has nothing to do with champagne — it comes from the fruit's
resemblance to the bubbles.

**Taste:** concentrated sweet grape with a firm acid edge
**Texture:** a lot of skin for the amount of flesh, so they pop rather than squash
**Look:** deep purple-black miniature bunches on fine stems

The mechanism is surface-to-volume ratio. Grape acidity and tannin live mostly in
the skin, and a tiny grape has far more skin per mouthful than a large one. That is
why champagne grapes taste sharper and more grape-like than table grapes, and why a
handful cuts through fat more effectively than the same weight of ordinary ones.

## What it pairs with — and why

| Pair it with | Why it works |
|---|---|
| **Brie** | The classic grape-and-bloomy-cheese pairing, in a form that looks like it took effort. |
| **Triple-crème** | Very rich cheese needs acid and water. Small sharp grapes are more effective than large sweet ones. |
| **Manchego** | Sheep salt and nuttiness with sweet fruit. A Spanish pairing that works with any small grape. |
| **Marcona almonds** | Fat, salt and sweet fruit. Scatter both across the same corner. |
| **Prosciutto** | Salt and fat cut by acid. Tuck a small bunch under a ruffle of ham. |
| **Ordinary grapes** | Two sizes of the same fruit reads as deliberate, and it gives the fruit corner scale. |

**Skip it with:** nothing in particular. Grapes are the least confrontational thing
on any board.

## How to prep it for the board

1. **Rinse and dry thoroughly.** Wet bunches make everything around them soggy.
2. **Cut into small bunches with scissors**, keeping the stems intact.
3. **Never pick them loose.** Loose champagne grapes look like spilled beads.
4. **Drape a bunch over the edge** of a cheese or across a gap — this is what they
   are for.
5. **Serve 1.5 oz per person**, about one small bunch each.
6. **Add them last** so the stems stay tidy.
7. **Fix:** shrivelled or dropping off the stem? They are past it. A fresh bunch is
   firm and the grapes stay attached when you lift it.

## What to buy

| Product | Where | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Champagne grapes** | Farmers markets, upscale grocers, late summer | The real thing. Short season, buy on sight. |
| **Small black seedless grapes** | Any grocery | The year-round substitute. Not as small, still good. |
| **Muscat grapes** | Specialty, autumn | Larger but intensely floral. A genuine upgrade in flavour. |
| **Concord grapes** | Farmers markets, autumn | Slip-skin and very aromatic. Seedy but memorable. |
| **Dried Zante currants** | Any grocery, baking aisle | The same fruit dried. Useful when the fresh ones are gone. |

**Label check:** buy by eye. The stems should be green and flexible, not brown and
brittle.

**Skip:** bunches with fruit already loose in the bottom of the punnet.

## Quick FAQ

**What are champagne grapes?**
Zante currants — tiny seedless grapes about the size of a peppercorn, sold in dense
small bunches. They have nothing to do with champagne.

**When are they in season?**
A short window in mid to late summer, roughly July to September. They are hard to
find outside it.

**Do they taste different from ordinary grapes?**
More concentrated and slightly more acidic, because the skin-to-flesh ratio is much
higher.

**How do you serve them?**
In whole small bunches, never picked loose. The bunch is the entire visual point.

**How much per person?**
About an ounce and a half, or one small bunch each.
